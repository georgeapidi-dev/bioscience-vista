STEP 1 · QUESTION

Why a bilayer forms

0 – 14 min · B2.1.1
  • A phospholipid has a hydrophilic phosphate head (attracted to water) and two hydrophobic hydrocarbon tails (repelled by water): it is amphipathic.
  • In water, phospholipids arrange themselves so that heads face the water on both sides and tails face each other inside: a bilayer. This needs no energy and no enzyme; it happens naturally.
  • Bilayers form continuous sheets that close into vesicles, so there are no exposed edges of hydrophobic tails.
  • Other amphipathic lipids (e.g. glycolipids, cholesterol) also sit in the bilayer.

STEP 2 · EXPLORE

A barrier

14 – 26 min · B2.1.2
  • The core of the membrane is made of hydrophobic hydrocarbon chains. Ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻) and polar molecules (glucose, amino acids) are attracted to water and cannot easily pass through this non-polar core.
  • Large molecules also pass very slowly. So the membrane has low permeability to large and hydrophilic particles.
  • This makes membranes effective barriers between two aqueous solutions: cytoplasm and tissue fluid, or cytoplasm and the inside of an organelle.

STEP 3 · TEST

Simple diffusion

26 – 40 min · B2.1.3
  • Simple diffusion is the net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to lower concentration, due to their random motion. It is passive: no ATP is used.
  • Oxygen and carbon dioxide are small and non-polar, so they diffuse directly between the phospholipids.
  • In alveoli, O₂ diffuses from air into blood and CO₂ from blood into air. In a respiring cell O₂ diffuses in because it is used up inside.
  • The rate increases with a steeper concentration gradient, larger surface area, shorter distance and higher temperature.
✏️ Worked example: Will it cross the bilayer by simple diffusion?
  1. Ask three questions about the particle: Is it small? Is it non-polar (or only slightly polar)? Is it uncharged?
  2. Oxygen, O₂: small, non-polar, uncharged, so it diffuses straight through the hydrophobic core.
  3. Sodium ion, Na⁺: small but charged, so it is surrounded by water molecules and repelled by the core; it needs a channel protein.
  4. Glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆: polar (many –OH groups) and fairly large, so it crosses very slowly by itself; it needs a carrier or channel protein.
  5. Rule: the more lipid-soluble and the smaller a molecule, the faster it crosses by simple diffusion.

STEP 4 · EXPLAIN

Membrane proteins

40 – 58 min · B2.1.4
  • Membrane proteins have very diverse structures, locations and functions.
  • Integral proteins are embedded in one or both lipid layers; many are transmembrane, spanning the whole bilayer, with hydrophobic amino acids in contact with the tails.
  • Peripheral proteins are attached to one surface of the bilayer (often to integral proteins), not embedded in it.
  • Functions: channels and pumps (transport), receptors for hormones, enzymes (e.g. in the mitochondrial inner membrane), cell-adhesion, recognition (glycoproteins), electron carriers.
