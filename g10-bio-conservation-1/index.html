A horse and a donkey can mate and produce a mule. Mules are strong and healthy, but a mule cannot have young of its own. That one fact tells biologists that horses and donkeys are separate species, and it is the key to understanding how new species appear.

By the end of this lesson I will be able to

  • State what a species is and explain how a population can become isolated.
  • Explain how isolated populations change until they are separate species.
  • Analyse evidence that speciation has happened.
  • A species is a group of organisms that can breed with one another to produce fertile offspring (offspring that can themselves have young).
  • A horse and a donkey produce a mule, but mules are infertile. So the horse and the donkey are two species. The mule is a hybrid.
  • Members of one species share most of their genes, but they are not identical: there is variation.
  • A population is all the members of one species living in one area.
  • Individuals of one population breed together, so their alleles are mixed in every generation. This keeps the population as one species.
  • Isolation means that two populations of a species are kept apart so that they cannot breed with each other.
  • The barrier is usually geographical: a new mountain range, a river that changes course, a canyon, rising sea level, or animals carried to an island.
  • The populations on each side no longer exchange alleles.
  • Example: squirrels on the two sides of the Grand Canyon; finches on different Galápagos islands; fish in lakes that were once joined.
  • Isolation alone does not make a new species. It only stops the mixing.
  • 1. A population is split by a barrier: isolation.
  • 2. Each population has variation, and new alleles appear by mutation.
  • 3. Conditions on the two sides are different (food, climate, predators), so natural selection favours different alleles on each side.
  • 4. Over many generations the two populations become more and more different in body, behaviour and DNA.
  • 5. In the end they can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring, even if they meet again. Speciation has taken place: there are now two species.
  • Darwin's finches: each Galápagos island has finches with beaks that suit the food there: thick beaks for hard seeds, thin beaks for insects. DNA shows they all descend from one ancestor species from the mainland.
  • Island species: islands often have species found nowhere else, which resemble those on the nearest mainland.
  • DNA: the more similar the DNA of two species, the more recently they separated.
  • Speciation seen happening: in fruit flies kept on different foods for many generations, the two groups came to prefer mates from their own group.
  • Speciation usually takes thousands to millions of years, which is why we rely on evidence of this kind.
The big ideaWhen populations of one species are kept apart, natural selection changes each in its own way until they can no longer interbreed: they are new species.

STEP 3 · TEST

Test: put speciation in order

25 – 40 min · Criteria A and C
  • Isolation → different selection → the populations change → they can no longer interbreed.
✏️ Worked example: Steps to a new species
  1. A river or mountain splits one population into two.
  2. Different conditions → different mutations and natural selection in each.
  3. Over many generations, the groups change in features and behaviour.
  4. If they meet again they cannot interbreed: two species.

Your answers

Q3. Put the steps in order. [5]
A barrier splits one population into two

Natural selection favours different alleles in each population
