Biology · Ecosystems · Lesson 2: Biotic and abiotic factors and how they interact

MYP 5 Sciences · Grade 10 · Biology · MYP 5 Ecosystems · Lesson 2 · Biology unit · lesson 2 of 4 · Grade 10 · Biology · 60 minutes · Prepared by VistaEd
Guiding question: Why do whistling-thorn acacias in Laikipia grow swollen thorns full of ants, and what happens to the grass under a tree when the rains fail?
Virtual labsGraph and drawing tools
🎯 Start here

In Laikipia, ants live inside the hollow thorns of whistling-thorn acacias. When an elephant starts to browse, hundreds of ants rush out and sting the inside of its trunk, so the elephant moves on. Where the ants have been lost, elephants strip the trees, more light reaches the ground and the whole community changes. Living and non-living factors are tied together.

By the end of this lesson I will be able to

  • Describe the biotic and abiotic factors of an ecosystem, with examples and how they are measured.
  • Explain how abiotic factors affect living organisms.
  • Explain how organisms change abiotic factors, and analyse data on these interactions.
  • Biotic factors are the living parts of an ecosystem and the effects living things have on one another.
  • Feeding roles: producers (plants, algae) make food by photosynthesis; consumers (herbivores such as zebras, carnivores such as lions) eat other organisms; decomposers (bacteria, fungi) break down dead material and return minerals to the soil; scavengers such as vultures and hyenas start the process.
  • Food supply is a biotic factor: when grass is plentiful after the rains, herbivore numbers rise; when it is scarce, they fall.
  • How species affect each other (competition, predation and symbiosis) is the next lesson in this topic: Relationships in ecosystems and population growth curves.
  • Other biotic factors: disease (rinderpest once killed most wildebeest), new species arriving (invasive water hyacinth on Lake Victoria) and human activity.
  • Abiotic factors are the non-living physical and chemical conditions of an ecosystem.
  • Light intensity (measured with a light meter): needed for photosynthesis; decides where plants grow.
  • Temperature (thermometer, temperature probe): affects enzymes and so the rate of growth; decides which species survive (alpine plants on Mount Kenya, corals only in warm seas).
  • Water / rainfall (rain gauge) and humidity (hygrometer): needed by all cells; deserts have few plants, rainforests many.
  • Soil: pH (universal indicator or pH meter), mineral ions such as nitrate, and texture. Wind (anemometer): increases water loss and spreads seeds. In water: dissolved oxygen (oxygen probe), salinity (salt content) and pH; Lake Nakuru is strongly alkaline and salty, so only specialised algae and animals live there.
  • Other abiotic factors: carbon dioxide concentration, altitude, slope and aspect, fire, floods.
  • Abiotic factors decide which species can live in a place and how many: each species has a range of conditions it can tolerate.
  • Light: in a forest, few plants grow on the dark floor; plants there have large thin leaves. Seaweed grows only in the top layers of the sea where light reaches.
  • Water: in the Maasai Mara, the grass grows after the rains; the wildebeest migrate to follow the fresh grass, and the lions and crocodiles follow the wildebeest.
  • Temperature: warmer seas cause coral bleaching (the corals lose their algae and may die), and all the fish that depend on the reef decline.
  • Salinity and pH: Lake Nakuru's salty, alkaline water allows huge blooms of the alga Arthrospira (spirulina), which feeds the flamingos; when heavy rains dilute the lake, the algae decline and the flamingos leave.
  • The effects go both ways: organisms change the abiotic environment.
  • Trees shade the ground, lowering light intensity and temperature and raising humidity beneath them; their roots hold soil and reduce erosion.
  • Decomposers add mineral ions such as nitrate to the soil; earthworms mix and aerate the soil and improve drainage.
  • Plants and algae release oxygen and take in carbon dioxide by photosynthesis; animals and decomposers use oxygen in respiration. Too much decay in water (after fertiliser run-off) can use up the dissolved oxygen so fish die.
  • Large animals change their surroundings: elephants push over trees, turning woodland into grassland with more light at ground level; hippos and buffalo trample paths and add dung that fertilises the water.
  • So an ecosystem is a web of interactions: a change in one factor (rain, a disease, the loss of ants on acacias) spreads through the whole community.
The big ideaLiving (biotic) and non-living (abiotic) factors affect each other: abiotic factors decide which organisms can live in a place, and organisms in turn change the abiotic conditions.

✏️ Worked example: Interpreting field data
  1. Under the canopy: light 200 lux, 5% grass cover.
  2. Open ground: light 20 000 lux, 80% grass cover.
  3. Positive correlation: more light, more grass.
  4. Explanation: grass needs light for photosynthesis; the tree changes the abiotic conditions below it.
