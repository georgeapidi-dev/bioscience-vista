STEP 1 · QUESTION

Phenotypic plasticity

0 – 12 min · D3.2.6
  • Phenotypic plasticity is the capacity of an organism to develop traits suited to the environment it experiences, by varying patterns of gene expression.
  • It is not due to changes in genotype, and changes may be reversible. Examples: skin tanning in sunlight; muscle growth with training; more red blood cells at high altitude (athletes training in Iten); arrowhead plants growing different leaf shapes under and above water.

STEP 2 · EXPLORE

Phenylketonuria

12 – 27 min · D3.2.7
  • PKU is caused by a recessive allele of an autosomal gene coding for the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase, which converts phenylalanine to tyrosine.
  • Homozygous recessive individuals lack functional enzyme; phenylalanine builds up and damages the developing brain. Heterozygotes (carriers) are unaffected.
  • Newborns are screened (heel-prick test); a low-phenylalanine diet prevents harm.

STEP 3 · TEST

Screening and diet

27 – 42 min · D3.2.7
  • A heel-prick blood test a few days after birth measures phenylalanine. Babies with PKU are put on a low-phenylalanine diet: no meat, fish, eggs, cheese, nuts or the sweetener aspartame.
  • Because the damage depends on diet, PKU also shows the interaction of genotype and environment: a child homozygous for the allele develops normally on the right diet.
  • Carriers (Pp) are unaffected because one functional allele makes enough phenylalanine hydroxylase.

STEP 4 · EXPLAIN

SNPs and multiple alleles

42 – 57 min · D3.2.8
  • New alleles arise by mutation; many differ by a single-nucleotide polymorphism.
  • Any number of alleles of a gene can exist in a gene pool (multiple alleles), but each diploid individual inherits only two.
