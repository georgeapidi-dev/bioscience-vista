3D tissue and organ printing

🎬 Videos for this lesson

▶ How to 3D print human tissue (TED-Ed)Four minutes on how bio-ink is printed layer by layer, and what stops us printing a whole organ.
  • 3D BIOPRINTING is a developing technology in which living cells and biological materials are deposited LAYER BY LAYER to build a tissue-like structure.
  • The "ink" is BIO-INK: living cells, often grown from stem cells, mixed with a HYDROGEL that holds them in position while they settle and join up.
  • The printer follows a digital plan, so the cells are placed in a controlled arrangement.
  • After printing, the structure is kept in an incubator so the cells can grow, attach to one another and start to behave as a tissue.
  • Current uses are mostly simple, flat or thin tissues (skin, cartilage), and tissue grown for research and for testing medicines instead of testing on animals or people.

STEP 2 · EXPLORE

Connecting stem cells and bioprinting

about 10 min · Explain
  • A TISSUE is a group of similar cells working together on one function. An ORGAN contains several different tissues, arranged in a particular way, working together.
  • So an organ needs several cell types printed in the right places, not one type printed in a block.
  • Every cell must be within about 0.2 mm of a BLOOD SUPPLY to receive oxygen and nutrients and have waste removed. A thick printed block dies in the middle unless blood vessels are built into it: this is the central problem in the field.
  • The cells must also communicate and function together: a printed heart must beat in a coordinated way, not just be heart-shaped.
  • The structure must be strong enough, sterile, and not REJECTED by the patient's immune system. Using the patient's own cells helps with rejection.

STEP 3 · TEST

Microscope skills and magnification

about 15 min · Practical
▶ How do you use a light microscope? A step-by-step guideWatch before you touch the microscope: lowest power, coarse focus, then fine focus.
  • 1. Carry the microscope with two hands: one on the arm, one under the base.
  • 2. Place the slide on the stage and hold it with the stage clips, specimen over the hole.
  • 3. Select the LOWEST power objective (×4) and turn on the lamp.
  • 4. Watching FROM THE SIDE, use the coarse focus to bring the objective close to the slide.
  • 5. Look through the eyepiece and turn the coarse focus the other way, raising the tube until the specimen appears. Never focus downwards while looking down the eyepiece.
  • 6. Sharpen with the fine focus. Centre the part you want before you change lenses.
  • 7. Turn the nosepiece to a higher power. Use the FINE focus only from now on.
  • 8. Record: specimen, magnification, and a scientific drawing.

Magnification

Total magnification. Total magnification = eyepiece × objective. A ×10 eyepiece with the ×40 objective gives 10 × 40 = ×400. Write the total, not the objective, next to your drawing
  • IMAGE SIZE (I): how big the specimen is in the drawing or photograph, measured with a ruler, usually in mm.
  • ACTUAL SIZE (A): how big the specimen really is. For cells this is usually in micrometres (µm).
  • MAGNIFICATION (M): how many times bigger the image is. It has NO unit: write ×400, not 400 mm.
  • The golden rule: I and A must be in the SAME unit before you divide. Convert first, every time.
Figure 4 · The I, A, M triangle
M = I ÷ A (magnification)
A = I ÷ M (actual size)
I = A × M (image size)
Cover the one you want. I and A must be in the SAME unit first.
Figure 5 · Converting units
cm ×10 → mm ×1000 → µm ×1000 → nm
cm ← ÷10 mm ← ÷1000 µm ← ÷1000 nm
Going down the scale (to smaller units) you multiply; going back up you divide.
Worked example
A photograph of a plant cell measures 45 mm across. The real cell is 90 µm wide.
Step 1 convert: 45 mm × 1000 = 45 000 µm · Step 2 M = I ÷ A = 45 000 ÷ 90 · Step 3 M = ×500 (no unit)
Check: a cell seen with a school microscope should come out between about ×100 and ×1000.

Recording an observation

Rules for a scientific drawing
Sharp pencil, single clean lines; no shading or colouring. Large: at least half the page width. Draw only what you really see. Ruled, horizontal label lines that touch the structure and do not cross. Underneath: the specimen name and the total magnification.

STEP 4 · EXPLAIN

Viruses

about 10 min · Explain
▶ Virus structure: capsid and genetic materialListen for capsid, genome and envelope.
  • A VIRUS is an infectious particle made of genetic material surrounded by a protein coat. It is not a cell, so it is described as NON-CELLULAR. One virus particle is a VIRION.
  • GENOME: the genetic material, DNA or RNA. It carries the instructions for making more virus particles, and nothing else.
  • CAPSID: the protein coat, built from repeating protein units. It protects the genome and helps the virus attach to a host cell.
  • ENVELOPE: some viruses (influenza, HIV) are wrapped in a membrane taken from the last host cell they left. Others (common cold viruses) have none.
  • SURFACE PROTEINS lock onto specific receptors on the host cell, so a virus usually infects only certain species and cell types.
  • A virus has no cytoplasm, no ribosomes and no metabolism. It must enter a HOST CELL and use its ribosomes, enzymes and raw materials to copy itself.
  • Viruses are about 100 nm across, far too small to see with a school light microscope.
