STEP 1 · QUESTION

Why twenty and not two?

5 min

A polypeptide could in principle be built from two kinds of amino acid instead of twenty. The chain would still form and the peptide bonds would be identical. But a protein made of two kinds of amino acid could not do very much. Everything a protein does comes from its shape, and its shape comes from the chemistry of the R groups.

STEP 2 · EXPLORE

R groups and their properties

17 min · B1.2.6
  • The backbone of every polypeptide is identical. All the chemical variety in a protein comes from the twenty R groups, and each falls into one of four types.
  • NON-POLAR R groups are hydrocarbons, such as the –CH(CH₃)₂ of valine. Carbon and hydrogen have almost the same electronegativity, so electrons are shared evenly, there are no partial charges and no hydrogen bonds can form with water. They are HYDROPHOBIC: pushed together into the interior of the protein.
  • POLAR, UNCHARGED R groups contain a strongly electronegative atom (usually oxygen or nitrogen), as in the –CH₂OH of serine, giving partial charges. They form hydrogen bonds with water and each other, so they are HYDROPHILIC.
  • CHARGED R groups carry a full charge at cellular pH. ACIDIC R groups end in a carboxyl that loses a proton to become –COO⁻ (aspartic acid, glutamic acid). BASIC R groups end in an amine that gains a proton to become –NH₃⁺ (lysine, arginine, histidine). Both are strongly HYDROPHILIC, and oppositely charged R groups attract to form ionic bonds.
  • CYSTEINE has a –CH₂SH R group; two cysteines can form a strong COVALENT disulfide bridge. GLYCINE has a single hydrogen as its R group, so it lets the chain turn very tightly.
  • Hydrophilic and hydrophobic follow directly from whether the R group can hydrogen bond with water. Polar and charged R groups can; non-polar cannot.

STEP 3 · TEST

Backbone, primary structure and conformation

18 min · B1.2.7
  • The BACKBONE of a polypeptide is the repeating chain of atoms formed by the condensation reactions: nitrogen, then the alpha carbon, then the carbonyl carbon, then nitrogen again: N–Cα–C. The R groups project from the alpha carbons and are not part of it.
  • The CONFORMATION of a protein is its precise three-dimensional shape: the arrangement in space of every atom. Function depends on conformation.
  • The PRIMARY STRUCTURE of a protein is the number and sequence of amino acids, counted from the N-terminal to the C-terminal. The bonding involved is the covalent PEPTIDE BOND only; primary structure says nothing about shape.
  • The sequence comes from DNA. The base sequence of a gene is transcribed into codons in mRNA, and each codon is translated into one amino acid at the ribosome.
  • Once the primary structure is fixed, the position of every R group is fixed too. The chain folds into the arrangement with the lowest free energy, the most stable one for that sequence. Because the sequence is always the same, the protein folds repeatedly and predictably into one conformation.
  • In sickle cell anaemia a glutamic acid (charged, hydrophilic, on the surface) is replaced by a valine (non-polar). The hydrophobic patch makes haemoglobin molecules stick together into fibres.

STEP 4 · EXPLAIN

Secondary structure

15 min · B1.2.8
  • SECONDARY STRUCTURE is regular, repeating folding of part of the polypeptide backbone, stabilised by HYDROGEN BONDS. Two forms: the alpha helix (coiling) and the beta pleated sheet (pleating).
  • The bonds are between the oxygen of the C=O group of one peptide bond and the hydrogen of the N–H group of another. Both atoms are in the BACKBONE, so secondary structure involves no R groups at all: the most examined distinction between secondary and tertiary structure.
  • The ALPHA HELIX is a right-handed coil with about 3.6 amino acids per turn. Each C=O hydrogen bonds to the N–H four amino acids further along, so the hydrogen bonds run roughly parallel to the helix axis. The R groups point outwards.
  • The BETA PLEATED SHEET forms when sections of chain lie alongside one another in an extended zig-zag. Hydrogen bonds form between neighbouring strands, at right angles to them. Strands may be parallel or antiparallel.
  • An individual hydrogen bond is weak, roughly a twentieth of a covalent bond. A secondary structure is stable because very many act together, which is also why heat disrupts it readily.
  • Most proteins contain both forms. Haemoglobin is almost entirely alpha helix; silk fibroin is almost entirely beta pleated sheet.

STEP 5 · CHALLENGE

Tertiary structure: four kinds of interaction

20 min · B1.2.9
  • TERTIARY STRUCTURE is the overall three-dimensional conformation of a single complete polypeptide, produced by interactions between R GROUPS that end up close together once the chain has folded, even if they are far apart in the primary structure.
  • HYDROGEN BONDS form between polar R groups, for example the –OH of serine and of threonine. Individually weak, but many.
  • IONIC BONDS form between a negatively charged R group (–COO⁻) and a positively charged one (–NH₃⁺). Stronger than hydrogen bonds, and broken when pH changes because the charges are removed.
  • DISULFIDE BRIDGES are strong COVALENT bonds between the sulfur atoms of two cysteine R groups. Not broken by heat or moderate pH. Proteins working outside the cell (keratin, insulin, antibodies) are rich in them.
  • HYDROPHOBIC INTERACTIONS are the clustering of non-polar R groups in the interior, away from water. Strictly not bonds: water bonds to itself and the non-polar groups are pushed together. A soluble protein has a hydrophobic core and hydrophilic surface.
  • Keep the levels straight. Secondary: hydrogen bonds only, backbone only. Tertiary: four kinds of interaction, R groups only.
