STEP 2 · EXPLORE

The repeating unit: one nucleotide

15 min · Explore

🎬 Videos for this lesson

▶ DNA nucleotides and bases (Cognito)What a nucleotide is made of, and what the four bases are. Watch before you answer Question 2.
  • DNA is a very long molecule, built from one small unit repeated over and over. That unit is the NUCLEOTIDE.
  • Every nucleotide has three parts: a PHOSPHATE group, a five-carbon SUGAR, and a BASE.
  • The phosphate and the sugar are the same in every DNA nucleotide. Only the BASE changes, which is why the base is the part that carries the information.
  • The sugar in DNA is DEOXYRIBOSE. The sugar in RNA is RIBOSE: DeoxyriboNucleic Acid and RiboNucleic Acid.
  • "Nucleic" tells you where it was first found: in the NUCLEUS of the cell.

STEP 3 · EXPLAIN

The four bases and how they pair

15 min · Explain
▶ DNA base pairing (Cognito)Why A goes with T and C goes with G. Watch, then answer Questions 3 and 4.
  • DNA uses four bases: ADENINE (A), THYMINE (T), CYTOSINE (C) and GUANINE (G).
  • A always pairs with T, and C always pairs with G. This is COMPLEMENTARY BASE PAIRING.
  • A and T are held together by TWO hydrogen bonds. C and G are held together by THREE.
  • The pairing is fixed by size and shape. A and G are large, two-ring bases; T and C are small, one-ring bases. A large one always pairs with a small one, so the two strands stay exactly the same distance apart.
  • Two large bases would not fit in the gap; two small bases would not reach across it. Neither would form the right hydrogen bonds.
  • If you know the bases on one strand, you can work out the other strand exactly.

STEP 4 · TEST

The polymer and the double helix

20 min · Explain · Elaborate
  • A POLYMER is a large molecule made of many small repeating units joined together. The small unit is a MONOMER.
  • In DNA the monomer is the nucleotide, and the polymer is a POLYNUCLEOTIDE. Starch is a polymer of glucose; protein is a polymer of amino acids.
  • Nucleotides join phosphate to sugar, phosphate to sugar, over and over: the SUGAR–PHOSPHATE BACKBONE.
  • The backbone is the same all the way along, so it does not carry any information. It is the scaffolding.
  • The bases stick out sideways from the backbone, where they can pair with the bases of the other strand.

5(c). Explain why the sugar–phosphate backbone cannot carry the genetic information. [1]
Support hintInformation needs something that varies. Does the backbone vary?
  • DNA is TWO polynucleotide strands, paired base to base and then twisted: the DOUBLE HELIX ("double" = two strands, "helix" = spiral).
  • Think of a twisted ladder: the backbones are the sides, each base pair is a rung. One full turn every ten base pairs.
  • The two strands are ANTIPARALLEL: side by side, but running in OPPOSITE directions, like the two carriageways of a road.
  • The strands are held together by HYDROGEN BONDS between paired bases: two between A and T, three between C and G.
  • One hydrogen bond is weak, but there are millions of them, so together they hold the molecule firmly.
  • Weak bonds are exactly what is needed: strong enough in bulk, but each one breaks easily, so DNA can be "unzipped" whenever the cell reads or copies it.

STEP 5 · CHALLENGE

Practice: DNA and RNA compared

10 min · Practice
  • RNA is also a nucleic acid built from nucleotides (phosphate, sugar and base). Three details differ.
  • RNA has ONE strand, not two, and is not twisted into a double helix.
  • The sugar in RNA is RIBOSE, not deoxyribose.
  • RNA uses URACIL (U) in place of thymine: RNA bases are A, U, C and G, and in RNA A pairs with U.
  • DNA is the master copy and stays safely in the nucleus. RNA is the working copy that carries the instructions out to the cytoplasm, where proteins are made.
