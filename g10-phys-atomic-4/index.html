Using radiation safely

Guiding question: The same radiation that can cause cancer is used to find it and to cure it. How do we decide when the benefit is worth the risk?

By the end of this lesson I will be able to

  • Describe uses of radioactive isotopes and explain the choice of isotope from its radiation and half-life.
  • Distinguish irradiation from contamination and describe safety precautions.
  • Describe nuclear fission and evaluate nuclear power.
  • Medical tracers: a small amount of a gamma emitter such as technetium-99m is injected or swallowed. A gamma camera outside the body follows where it goes.
  • Radiotherapy: beams of gamma rays are aimed at a tumour from several directions to kill cancer cells.
  • Sterilising: gamma rays kill bacteria on surgical instruments and on food, without heating them.
  • Smoke alarms: an alpha source (americium-241) ionises the air so that a small current flows. Smoke absorbs the alpha particles, the current falls and the alarm sounds.
  • Thickness control: a beta source and a detector on either side of paper or metal foil in a factory. If the sheet gets thicker, fewer beta particles get through and the rollers are adjusted.
  • Two questions decide the choice: which radiation, and what half-life?
  • Medical tracer: a gamma emitter, because gamma passes out of the body to the camera and is only weakly ionising; with a short half-life (hours), so that the patient is not radioactive for long.
  • Smoke alarm: an alpha emitter, because alpha is strongly ionising and is stopped by smoke and by the plastic case; with a long half-life (americium-241: 432 years), so that the alarm keeps working for years.
  • Thickness of paper: a beta emitter. Alpha would be stopped completely by the paper; gamma would pass through whatever its thickness. A long half-life keeps the count steady.
  • Sterilising: a gamma emitter with a long half-life, because gamma penetrates through the packaging.
  • Ionising radiation can damage or kill cells and can change DNA, causing mutations and cancer. The larger the dose, the greater the risk.
  • Irradiation: an object is exposed to radiation from a source outside it. It does not become radioactive, and the exposure stops when the source is removed.
  • Contamination: radioactive material gets on or into the object. It keeps exposing the object until it is removed or has decayed.
  • Three rules reduce the dose: time (as short as possible), distance (use tongs; stand back) and shielding (lead screens, lead-lined containers).
  • People who work with radiation wear a badge that records their dose.
  • Nuclear fission is the splitting of a large nucleus, such as uranium-235, into two smaller nuclei. It is started when the nucleus absorbs a neutron.
  • Each fission releases a large amount of energy and two or three more neutrons. These can split more nuclei: a chain reaction.
  • In a reactor the chain reaction is controlled by control rods that absorb neutrons. The energy heats water to steam, which turns a turbine and a generator.
  • Advantages: no carbon dioxide is released while it runs; a small mass of fuel gives a very large amount of energy; a reliable, steady supply.
  • Disadvantages: radioactive waste stays hazardous for thousands of years and must be stored safely; accidents are rare but serious; stations are costly to build and to take apart.
The big ideaRadiation is useful and hazardous; choosing the right isotope and the right precautions lets us take the benefit while limiting the risk.

STEP 2 · EXPLORE

Choosing an isotope

10 – 25 min · Criterion A

✏️ Worked example: Choosing an isotope
  1. Job: tracer to image the thyroid.
  2. Needs gamma (escapes the body to the camera).
  3. Needs a short half-life (hours) to limit the dose.
  4. Iodine-123 or technetium-99m fits.
