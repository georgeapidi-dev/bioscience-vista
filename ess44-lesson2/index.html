STEP 1 · QUESTION

Nutrient inputs

0 – 10 min · 4.4.5
  • Nitrates: fertilizer runoff and leaching, manure, sewage. Phosphates: fertilizer, detergents, sewage, soil erosion.
  • Nutrients are usually limiting in natural waters, so adding them triggers rapid phytoplankton growth.
  • Most eutrophication is anthropogenic (cultural eutrophication).

STEP 2 · EXPLORE

The sequence

10 – 25 min · 4.4.6
  • 1 Nutrients enter → 2 phytoplankton bloom → 3 turbidity rises, light blocked → 4 submerged plants die → 5 algae die → 6 bacteria decompose dead matter, using oxygen (high BOD) → 7 hypoxia/anoxia → 8 fish and invertebrates die → more decomposition (positive feedback) → 9 anaerobic bacteria release methane and hydrogen sulfide.
✏️ Worked example: Day and night oxygen
  1. Daytime: algae photosynthesise, DO 12 mg L⁻¹.
  2. Night: algae and bacteria respire only.
  3. Dawn: DO falls to 1 mg L⁻¹.
  4. Fish kills often happen at dawn.

STEP 3 · TEST

Impacts on ecosystem services

25 – 40 min · 4.4.7
  • Fisheries: fish kills, loss of valuable species. Recreation and tourism: smelly, green water; swimming bans. Aesthetics: scum, smell.
  • Health: toxic algal blooms, higher water treatment costs, nitrate in drinking water. Biodiversity: loss of sensitive species; dead zones (Gulf of Mexico, Baltic).

STEP 4 · EXPLAIN

Managing eutrophication: three levels

40 – 55 min · 4.4.8
  • 1 Alter human activity: precision fertilizer use, organic farming, phosphate-free detergents, less meat.
  • 2 Control release: buffer strips of vegetation along rivers, sewage treatment with nutrient removal, constructed wetlands.
  • 3 Clean up/restore: dredge phosphate-rich sediment, remove algae and hyacinth, aerate water, reintroduce plants and fish.
  • Level 1 is most effective long-term; level 3 is costly and treats symptoms.
