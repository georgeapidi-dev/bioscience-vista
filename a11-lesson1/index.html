STEP 1 · QUESTION

Water as the medium for life

8 min · A1.1.1

Notes: read this before you answer

  • The first cells originated in water. The earliest evidence of life comes from rocks that formed in ancient oceans, and every cell alive today is still mostly water.
  • Water was where life could begin because chemical reactions happen in solution, because water absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation from the young Sun, and because the temperature of a large body of water changes only slowly.
  • Life still depends on water for the same reasons: it dissolves the substances cells need, it is liquid over the range of temperatures at which organisms live, it takes a great deal of energy to heat or cool, it is a reactant in reactions such as photosynthesis and hydrolysis, and ice floats, so a frozen lake insulates the living water beneath it.

STEP 2 · EXPLORE

Atoms, bonds and the water molecule

20 min · A1.1.2

Notes, part one: atoms

  • An atom has a nucleus containing protons (charge +1) and neutrons (no charge). Electrons (charge −1) move around the nucleus in shells.
  • An atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so overall it is neutral.
  • The outer shell decides how an atom bonds. An atom is stable when its outer shell is full: two electrons for hydrogen, eight for oxygen.

Notes, part two: how atoms bond

  • In an IONIC bond, one atom transfers an electron to another. Both then have full outer shells, but they are no longer neutral: they have become ions. A positive ion is a cation, a negative ion is an anion, and the opposite charges attract.
  • In a COVALENT bond, two atoms share a pair of electrons instead. The shared pair is attracted to both nuclei at once, and that shared attraction holds the atoms together.
  • Water is covalent. Oxygen needs two more electrons, and each hydrogen needs one, so one oxygen bonds with two hydrogens.

4(b). Outline how a covalent bond forms between an oxygen atom and a hydrogen atom. [2]
Support hintSay what the atoms do with their electrons. Say what holds the atoms together, or why oxygen forms two bonds.

Notes, part three: why water is polar

  • The two covalent bonds in a water molecule are the two O–H bonds, between the oxygen and each hydrogen.
  • Oxygen attracts the shared electrons much more strongly than hydrogen does. The shared pair is not held equally: it sits nearer the oxygen. A covalent bond like this is called a POLAR covalent bond.
  • The result is that the oxygen carries a partial negative charge, written δ−, and each hydrogen a partial positive charge, written δ+. Because the molecule is bent, these charges do not cancel out, so the whole molecule is polar.
  • A HYDROGEN BOND forms when the δ+ hydrogen of one water molecule is attracted to the δ− oxygen of a neighbouring molecule. One hydrogen bond is weak (about one twentieth the strength of a covalent bond) but in liquid water each molecule is bonded to several others, and there are enormous numbers of them.
  • That collective strength is why water is a liquid rather than a gas at the temperatures at which life occurs, why so much energy is needed to heat it or evaporate it, and why a column of water can be held together under tension in the xylem of a tall tree.

STEP 3 · TEST

Cohesion

13 min · A1.1.3

Notes: read this before you answer

  • COHESION is the attraction between molecules of the same substance. In water it is caused by hydrogen bonding between water molecules.
  • Cohesion is what allows water to be pulled up a tall plant. Water evaporates from the leaves and is lost through the stomata (transpiration), which creates a tension at the top of the xylem. Because the water molecules are hydrogen bonded to each other, the whole column is pulled up as one continuous thread and does not break.
  • SURFACE TENSION is a second consequence. A water molecule in the body of the liquid is pulled equally in every direction by its neighbours. A molecule at the surface has no water above it, so it is pulled sideways and downwards only. The surface molecules are therefore drawn together into a layer that behaves like a thin elastic skin.
  • That skin is strong enough for pond skaters and other small invertebrates to stand and move on the surface of a pond.

STEP 4 · EXPLAIN

Adhesion and capillary action

15 min · A1.1.4

Notes: read this before you answer

  • ADHESION is the attraction between water molecules and a different substance. Water adheres to anything that is polar or charged.
  • POLAR means a molecule has an uneven distribution of charge, so one part is slightly negative and another slightly positive. An ION is an atom, or group of atoms, that has gained or lost electrons and therefore carries a full electrical charge: a cation if positive, an anion if negative.
  • Because water itself is polar, its δ− oxygen is attracted to positive charges and its δ+ hydrogens to negative charges. That is why water sticks to cellulose in a plant cell wall, to glass, and to the surfaces of soil particles, but not to a waxy leaf surface, which is non-polar.
  • CAPILLARY ACTION is adhesion and cohesion working together in a narrow space. Adhesion pulls water along the wall of the tube; cohesion drags the rest of the column up behind it. The narrower the tube, the higher the water rises.
  • In a plant, capillary action helps water move up the very narrow xylem vessels and through the cell walls of the leaf to the evaporating surfaces.
  • In soil, the spaces between the particles act as narrow tubes. Water adheres to the particles and is drawn upwards through the spaces, so a plant can reach water that lies below its roots. In dry weather the same process carries water to the surface, where it evaporates and is lost.
