STEP 1 · QUESTION

Cyclins

0 – 16 min · D2.1.15
  • Cyclins are proteins whose concentrations rise and fall during the cell cycle. Different cyclins peak at different stages.
  • A cell can pass each checkpoint (e.g. G1→S, G2→M, metaphase→anaphase) only when the concentration of a specific cyclin reaches a threshold level. Cyclins bind and activate cyclin-dependent kinases, which trigger the next stage.
  • After the checkpoint, the cyclin is broken down, so the cycle runs in one direction. You do not need the roles of specific cyclins.

STEP 2 · EXPLORE

Oncogenes and tumour suppressors

16 – 32 min · D2.1.16
  • Proto-oncogenes are normal genes that stimulate cell division (accelerators). A mutation can convert one into an oncogene, which is permanently active and keeps stimulating division.
  • Tumour suppressor genes (e.g. TP53, coding p53) inhibit division or trigger repair or apoptosis if DNA is damaged (brakes). A mutation that inactivates both copies removes the brake.
  • Usually several mutations in both types of gene are needed before division becomes uncontrolled, which is why cancer risk rises with age and with exposure to mutagens (UV, tobacco smoke).

STEP 3 · TEST

Tumours

32 – 46 min · D2.1.17
  • A tumour is a mass of cells formed by uncontrolled division.
  • Benign tumours grow slowly, stay in one place (often encapsulated), do not invade neighbouring tissue and are not cancer (e.g. most moles, fibroids), though they may press on organs.
  • Malignant tumours (cancers) divide rapidly, invade neighbouring tissues and can undergo metastasis: cells break away (losing adhesion), travel in blood or lymph and form secondary tumours elsewhere. The original is the primary tumour.

STEP 4 · EXPLAIN

The mitotic index

46 – 60 min · D2.1.17
  • Mitotic index = number of cells in mitosis ÷ total number of cells observed (often × 100 for a percentage).
  • A high mitotic index indicates rapid cell division; pathologists use it to assess how aggressive a tumour is.
  • Count cells in prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase as “in mitosis”.
