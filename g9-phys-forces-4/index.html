Gravitational fields and weight

An astronaut in a spacesuit can bound across the Moon carrying equipment that she could barely lift on Earth. She has not lost any mass. What has changed is the strength of the gravitational field pulling on her.

By the end of this lesson I will be able to

  • Distinguish between mass and weight and calculate weight using W = mg.
  • Describe a gravitational field and how its strength varies with distance.
  • Compare gravitational field strength on different planets.
  • Mass: the amount of matter in an object, in kg. It is the same everywhere.
  • Weight: the force of gravity on an object, in newtons (N). It changes with location.
  • Weight is measured with a newton meter (spring balance); mass with a balance.
  • Weight is a vector: it acts towards the centre of the planet.
  • Weight = mass × gravitational field strength: W = mg.
  • On Earth g ≈ 10 N/kg (9.8 N/kg): a 60 kg student weighs 600 N.
  • On the Moon g ≈ 1.6 N/kg: the same student weighs 96 N.
  • g is also the acceleration of free fall: 10 m/s² near the Earth (ignoring air resistance).
  • A gravitational field is a region where a mass feels a force.
  • Every mass has a field; larger masses have stronger fields.
  • Field lines point towards the centre of the planet; closer lines mean a stronger field.
  • The field gets weaker with distance from the planet.
  • Field strength (N/kg): Moon 1.6, Mars 3.7, Earth 9.8, Jupiter 24.8.
  • A larger, more massive planet has a stronger field at its surface.
  • Astronauts in orbit still have weight: they feel "weightless" because they are falling around the Earth.
The big ideaMass is the amount of matter and does not change; weight is the gravitational force on that mass and depends on the field strength: W = mg.

✏️ Worked example: Weight on another world
  1. A rover has mass 900 kg.
  2. On Earth: W = m × g = 900 × 9.8 = 8820 N.
  3. On Mars: W = 900 × 3.7 = 3330 N.
  4. Its mass is 900 kg everywhere; only its weight changes.
