A room thermostat switches the heater on when the room is cold and off when it is warm, keeping the temperature steady. Your body has hundreds of these control systems. They keep your temperature, blood sugar and water at just the right level, every second of your life. When they fail, you become ill.

By the end of this lesson I will be able to

  • Define homeostasis and explain why it is needed.
  • Describe the parts of a control system and explain negative feedback with examples.
  • Explain how nerves and hormones both take part in homeostasis.
  • Homeostasis is the maintenance of a constant internal environment (a steady state, or equilibrium) in the body, despite changes inside and outside.
  • Conditions kept within narrow limits include: body temperature (about 37 °C), blood glucose concentration (about 4–7 mmol/L, or 0.9 g/L), water content of the blood, carbon dioxide and pH.
  • Why? Enzymes work best at about 37 °C and the right pH; cells need a steady supply of glucose for respiration; cells swell or shrink if the water balance is wrong.
  • Healthy wellbeing is the consequence of these systems working properly (the statement of inquiry).
  • Every homeostatic control system has the same parts:
  • Set point: the normal value (e.g. 37 °C).
  • Receptor: detects a change from the set point (e.g. temperature receptors in the skin and the brain).
  • Coordinator (control centre): receives the information and decides on a response (e.g. the hypothalamus in the brain, or the pancreas for glucose).
  • Effector: makes the change that brings the value back (muscles, sweat glands, the liver).
  • Information travels by nerve impulses (fast) or hormones from glands (slower, longer-lasting).
  • In negative feedback, a change from the set point causes a response that reverses the change, bringing the value back towards the set point.
  • Too high → mechanisms lower it. Too low → mechanisms raise it. So the value fluctuates around the set point.
  • Example (temperature): body too hot → receptors detect → hypothalamus → sweat glands secrete sweat and skin blood vessels widen → body cools → back to 37 °C → sweating stops.
  • Nerves and glands work together: the hypothalamus (part of the nervous system) controls the pituitary gland (an endocrine gland), which releases hormones such as ADH.
The big ideaHomeostasis is keeping the internal environment of the body constant; it works by negative feedback, in which a change from the set point is detected and effectors reverse it, using nerves and hormones.

STEP 3 · TEST

Test: negative feedback

25 – 40 min · Criteria A and C
  • A change is detected → effectors reverse the change → back to the set point.
✏️ Worked example: A negative feedback loop
  1. Set point: blood glucose about 5 mmol/L.
  2. Change: after a meal glucose rises.
  3. Receptor/coordinator: pancreas detects it → releases insulin.
  4. Effector: liver and muscles take up glucose → level falls back to the set point.

Your answers
