Thermal physics and internal energy

MYP 4 Sciences · Grade 9 · Physics · MYP 4 Energy, pressure and thermal physics · Lesson 4 · Physics unit · lesson 4 of 5 · Grade 9 · Physics · 60 minutes · Prepared by BioscienceVista
Guiding question: What is actually happening to the particles when something gets hot?

A cup of chai cools on the table; an ice cube melts in your hand; a car tyre gets hot after a long drive. Each time, the energy stored in the moving and vibrating particles, the internal energy, is changing.

By the end of this lesson I will be able to

  • Describe internal energy and distinguish between heat (energy transferred) and temperature.
  • Use the particle model to explain changes of state.
  • Interpret heating and cooling curves.
  • The particles of a substance have kinetic energy (they move or vibrate) and potential energy (from the forces between them).
  • Internal energy = the total kinetic and potential energy of all the particles.
  • Heating a substance increases its internal energy.
  • Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of the particles (°C or K).
  • Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference.
  • A bath of warm water has more internal energy than a cup of boiling water, but a lower temperature.
  • At absolute zero (0 K = −273 °C) particles have the least possible kinetic energy. K = °C + 273.
  • Solid: particles vibrate in fixed positions. Liquid: close together, move around. Gas: far apart, move fast.
  • Melting and boiling: energy breaks the forces between particles.
  • During a change of state the temperature stays constant: energy increases potential energy, not kinetic energy.
  • A heating curve has sloping parts (temperature rising) and flat parts (changing state).
  • First flat part: melting point. Second: boiling point.
  • A cooling curve is the reverse; the flat parts show freezing and condensing.
The big ideaInternal energy is the total energy of the particles; heating raises the temperature by making particles move faster, except during a change of state, when the energy breaks the forces between particles instead.

STEP 4 · EXPLAIN

Explain: why boiling takes longer

40 – 52 min · Criterion A
  • The flat part at 100 °C was much longer than the flat part at 0 °C.
✏️ Worked example: Reading a heating curve
  1. Section 1: temperature rises: solid particles vibrate faster (KE increases).
  2. Section 2: flat at 0 °C: melting; energy breaks bonds (internal PE increases).
  3. Section 3: rises again: liquid warms.
  4. Section 4: flat at 100 °C: boiling; the longest flat section, because more energy is needed to separate particles completely.
