Applications of radiation

Guiding question: How can something dangerous save lives?

Kenyatta National Hospital treats cancer with beams of radiation. Some smoke alarms contain a tiny radioactive source. Paper mills check the thickness of paper with beta radiation. The same radiation that can cause harm is used every day to diagnose disease, treat cancer and keep people safe.

By the end of this lesson I will be able to

  • Describe uses of radiation in medicine and industry, including thickness control and smoke alarms.
  • Explain the choice of radiation for each use.
  • Evaluate the benefits and risks of using radiation.
  • Radiotherapy: beams of gamma rays (or X-rays) from several directions meet at a tumour, killing cancer cells while limiting damage to healthy tissue.
  • Tracers: a gamma emitter with a short half-life is injected; a camera outside the body follows it (e.g. PET scans).
  • Sterilisation: gamma rays kill bacteria on sealed medical equipment.
  • Thickness control: beta radiation passes through paper or foil; if the count falls, the sheet is too thick and the rollers adjust.
  • Smoke alarms: alpha particles ionise air in a chamber, making a small current; smoke absorbs the alpha particles, the current falls and the alarm sounds.
  • Leak detection: a gamma tracer in a buried pipe shows where it leaks.
  • Inside the body: gamma (it passes out to be detected and is weakly ionising) with a short half-life.
  • Thickness of paper: beta (alpha would be stopped; gamma would pass through almost unchanged).
  • Smoke alarm: alpha (strongly ionising, stopped by smoke, and does not escape the alarm).
  • Irradiation: being exposed to radiation from outside. The object does not become radioactive.
  • Contamination: radioactive material gets onto or into something; it keeps exposing it.
  • Protection: keep exposure short, stay far away, use shielding (time, distance, shielding).
The big ideaEach use of radiation depends on how penetrating and ionising it is; the benefits of medical and industrial uses are weighed against the risks, and exposure is kept as low as possible.

Test: the smoke alarm

✏️ Worked example: Choosing a radiation for a job
  1. Job: check the thickness of aluminium foil on a production line.
  2. Alpha: stopped completely by foil → no use.
  3. Gamma: passes through almost unchanged → small changes not detected.
  4. Beta: partly absorbed; count rate changes with thickness → choose beta.
