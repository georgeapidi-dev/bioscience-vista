STEP 1 · QUESTION

One rule, three different answers

5 min

An enzyme floating in the cytoplasm, a receptor embedded in the plasma membrane, and a channel letting potassium ions through that membrane are all folded by the same rule: non-polar R groups end up away from water, polar and charged R groups end up in contact with it. But the three proteins sit in different surroundings: one in water, one in the oily middle of a membrane, and one in both at once. The same rule therefore produces three different arrangements.

STEP 2 · EXPLORE

Soluble globular proteins

15 min · B1.2.10
  • An amino acid is HYDROPHOBIC if its R group is non-polar, and HYDROPHILIC if its R group is polar or charged. This decides where each amino acid ends up when the chain folds.
  • Most enzymes, plasma proteins and hormones are dissolved in an aqueous medium. As the chain folds, hydrophobic amino acids are pushed into the INTERIOR, forming a hydrophobic core; hydrophilic amino acids end up on the OUTER SURFACE, hydrogen bonding with water.
  • Consequences: the molecule is SOLUBLE (its surface interacts with water, so it dissolves and can be transported), and it is COMPACT and roughly spherical (globular), because tucking the hydrophobic residues inside pulls the chain into a ball.
  • The active site of an enzyme, or binding site of a hormone or antibody, is usually a cleft in that surface with a precise arrangement of R groups: that makes binding specific.
  • The sequence determines which residues are hydrophobic and where they are, and that determines how the chain must fold to bury them. The arrangement is a consequence of the primary structure.

STEP 3 · TEST

Membrane and channel proteins

18 min · B1.2.10
  • The middle of a phospholipid bilayer is made of NON-POLAR fatty acid tails, so a protein sitting in it faces the opposite environment to one in the cytoplasm: the arrangement is reversed.
  • INTEGRAL MEMBRANE PROTEINS: the membrane-spanning part has HYDROPHOBIC amino acids on its outer surface, in contact with the tails (often an alpha helix of about twenty non-polar amino acids). Where it emerges into the cytoplasm or extracellular fluid, the surface amino acids are HYDROPHILIC.
  • This anchors the protein (pulling it out would drag hydrophobic residues into water) and fixes its ORIENTATION: a receptor cannot end up facing the wrong way.
  • CHANNEL PROTEINS span the membrane in the same way, but have a PORE through the middle LINED WITH HYDROPHILIC amino acids. The lining holds a column of water, so ions and polar molecules pass through by facilitated diffusion.
  • Remember: soluble globular protein, hydrophilic outside and hydrophobic inside; channel protein in the membrane, hydrophobic outside and hydrophilic inside.

STEP 4 · EXPLAIN

Quaternary structure

14 min · B1.2.11
  • QUATERNARY STRUCTURE exists when a functional protein is made of TWO OR MORE polypeptide chains (subunits), held by the same interactions as tertiary structure (hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bridges, hydrophobic interactions), not peptide bonds.
  • A protein with only one chain has no quaternary structure: lysozyme and pepsin stop at tertiary.
  • NON-CONJUGATED proteins are polypeptide only. Insulin: two chains, A and B, held by disulfide bridges. Collagen: three chains.
  • CONJUGATED proteins contain a non-polypeptide component, the PROSTHETIC GROUP. Haemoglobin: four chains (two alpha, two beta), each with a haem group holding one Fe²⁺, which binds the oxygen.
  • The prosthetic group is usually essential: haemoglobin without haem carries no oxygen. Other conjugated proteins: glycoproteins, lipoproteins, cytochrome c.
  • Quaternary structure is about the NUMBER OF CHAINS. Conjugation is about whether anything NOT a chain is present. Insulin has quaternary structure and is non-conjugated.

STEP 5 · CHALLENGE

Form and function: collagen and insulin

23 min · B1.2.12

Fibrous proteins: collagen

  • Long and narrow, with a repetitive sequence, and insoluble. Structural roles; stable over a wide range of temperature and pH.
  • COLLAGEN is the most abundant protein in the human body. About one amino acid in three is GLYCINE, and the sequence repeats as glycine–proline–X.
  • Three chains wind into a TRIPLE HELIX, held by hydrogen bonds between the chains. Glycine is the only amino acid small enough (R group a single H) to sit at the crowded centre where the three chains touch.
  • Triple helices line up in a STAGGERED arrangement with covalent CROSS-LINKS, building fibrils and fibres, with no line of weakness where the ends meet.
  • The result is very high TENSILE STRENGTH: collagen resists being pulled apart. It is in tendons, ligaments, skin, artery walls, bone and the cornea.
▶ Fibrous proteins: collagen, keratin and elastinFive minutes. Watch after drafting question 7.

Globular proteins: insulin

  • Roughly spherical, compact, irregular sequence, soluble. Metabolic roles: enzymes, hormones, transport, antibodies. Function depends on a precise shape with a specific binding site.
  • INSULIN is a hormone made in the beta cells of the islets of Langerhans. 51 amino acids in two chains: A (21) and B (30), held by two disulfide bridges. Small, with quaternary structure, non-conjugated.
  • Hydrophilic R groups on its surface make it SOLUBLE in plasma, so it is carried to target cells all over the body.
  • Its conformation is COMPLEMENTARY to the insulin receptor on target cells (liver, muscle, fat), so it affects them and nothing else. That is SPECIFICITY.

If you finish early: aquaporins (not marked)

An aquaporin is a channel protein that lets water cross a membrane far faster than through the bilayer alone. At its narrowest point the pore is lined with two positively charged arginine R groups. Water crosses freely; H⁺ ions, which are much smaller than a water molecule, do not.
