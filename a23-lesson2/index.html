  • Genetic material: DNA or RNA, single- or double-stranded.
  • With or without a lipid envelope.
  • Capsid shapes: helical (threadlike), polyhedral (e.g. icosahedral), spherical, or complex (head and tail).
  • Host specificity: attachment proteins fit receptors on particular cells, e.g. HIV infects helper T cells; hepatitis viruses infect liver cells.
  • A virus of the bacterium Escherichia coli.
  • Double-stranded DNA packed in an icosahedral capsid head.
  • A long, flexible, non-contractile tail ending in a tail fibre.
  • When the tail fibre binds a receptor on the bacterial surface, the DNA passes through the tail into the cell; the capsid stays outside.
  • Cause respiratory diseases in mammals and birds; spread in respiratory droplets and aerosols.
  • Single-stranded RNA genome; roughly spherical.
  • A lipid envelope outside the capsid.
  • Many spike glycoproteins project from the surface, giving the "crown" (corona) look; the spike binds the host receptor.
  • Examples: SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19), SARS and MERS.
  • Spread only by exchange of body fluids: unprotected sex, infected blood, shared needles, and from mother to child (placenta, birth, breast milk).
  • Two single strands of RNA and the enzyme reverse transcriptase, which makes DNA from the viral RNA: HIV is a retrovirus.
  • A protein capsid, inside an envelope taken from the host helper T cell's membrane, with glycoproteins as attachment proteins.

Every virus has the same job: get its genes into a cell and get copies out. Yet some carry DNA and some RNA; some are rods, some are icosahedra, some have tails; some wear a stolen membrane. Why might so many designs work?

STEP 4 · EXPLAIN

Why so much diversity?

35 – 50 min · Teacher-guided explanation
  • Different genome types need different enzymes: RNA viruses must bring or encode an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, because host cells do not copy RNA from RNA.
  • Retroviruses bring reverse transcriptase so their genome can become DNA and hide in the host genome.
  • Segmented genomes (influenza) allow whole genes to be swapped between strains (reassortment).
  • Enveloped viruses leave cells by budding without killing them at once; many non-enveloped viruses leave by bursting (lysis).
