STEP 1 · QUESTION

The journey from pollen to seed

0 – 12 min · Watch first · shared vocabulary
Have you ever wondered…
Behind every fruit you eat is a hidden journey. A grain of pollen no wider than a hair must reach exactly the right place on exactly the right flower, carried by wind, an insect or a bird. Then two cells fuse, and the flower turns itself into something else. And then the seed has to leave.

▶ Seed and Fruit DispersalThe main dispersal methods and why plants need them (Groups 2, 3).
▶ Seed Dispersal 1Examples of seed and fruit adaptations for each method (Groups 2, 3, 5).
▶ Seed Dispersal 2More examples, including distances travelled (Groups 3, 4, 5).
  • POLLINATION is the TRANSFER of pollen from an anther to a stigma. Nothing has joined yet: it is a journey.
  • FERTILISATION is the FUSION of the male gamete with the female gamete inside the ovule, making a zygote. It happens later, and only if pollination succeeded.
  • SELF-pollination: onto a stigma of the same plant. CROSS-pollination: onto a different plant of the same species, which gives more variation.
  • After fertilisation: the ZYGOTE becomes the EMBRYO, the OVULE becomes the SEED, the OVARY becomes the FRUIT. Petals, stamens, style and stigma wither.
  • DISPERSAL is the movement of seeds or fruits away from the parent plant: a separate stage again.

  • The pollen grain lands on the STIGMA and germinates.
  • A POLLEN TUBE grows down through the STYLE and enters the ovule through the MICROPYLE.
  • The MALE GAMETE travels down the tube and FUSES with the female gamete in the ovule: a ZYGOTE.
  • The zygote divides by mitosis into the EMBRYO; the ovule wall becomes the TESTA (seed coat).
  • Cross-pollination is usually better for the species: more variation, so some offspring survive a change in conditions.

  • WHY DISPERSAL MATTERS: seedlings beneath the parent compete with it and with each other for LIGHT, WATER, MINERAL IONS and SPACE, and most die.

STEP 4 · EXPLAIN

Group 3 · Which method works best?

group work 12 – 32 min · Evaluate
  • WIND: long distance, no other organism needed, each seed cheap; but completely random, so most land somewhere unsuitable and the plant must make thousands.
  • ANIMAL, EATEN: seeds may travel kilometres and land in fertiliser, so fewer are needed; but sugars and flesh cost a lot of energy, and the method fails if the animal disappears.
  • ANIMAL, ATTACHED: cheap (no food reward) and fairly reliable; but the distance is unpredictable and the seed may be groomed off at once.
  • WATER: can carry a seed thousands of kilometres, but only if the plant grows beside water.
  • EXPLOSIVE: cheap and independent, but the distance is very small, so competition is reduced rather than avoided.
The judgement. There is no single best method. Success depends on the habitat, the animals present, and the trade-off between many cheap seeds and few expensive, well-delivered ones

STEP 5 · CHALLENGE

Groups 4 and 5 · Farming, conservation and investigation

group work 12 – 32 min · Discuss and design

Group 4 · Why this matters outside the classroom

  • AGRICULTURE: keep bees near a crop and never spray insecticide while it is flowering, or yield collapses. Mango, avocado, passion fruit and coffee are insect-pollinated; maize is wind-pollinated, so plant it in a block, not a single row.
  • Where no pollinator exists, flowers are pollinated by hand (vanilla). Weeds such as blackjack spread on livestock and clothing, so cleaning both limits them.
  • CONSERVATION: a tree dispersed only by large fruit-eating animals cannot spread once those animals are hunted out. Seed banks preserve old varieties. Water hyacinth and lantana spread aggressively because they disperse so well.
  • Which species to protect, who pays, and whether a farmer should lose income to save a pollinator are questions biology can inform but cannot settle.
