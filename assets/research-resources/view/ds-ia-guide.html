What the inquiry project is, how it is marked, and how to plan it stage by stage.

By George Apidi

The essentials

Requirements: IB Digital society guide (first assessment 2024). Inquiry project: 30 hours; 24 marks; 30% at SL and 20% at HL; an inquiry process document (maximum 1,500 words), a recorded multimedia presentation (maximum 10 minutes) and a list of references. Criteria: A Inquiry focus 3, B Claims and perspectives 6 (document); C Analysis and evaluation 6, D Conclusion 6, E Communication 3 (presentation).

It is an individual project. You lead it (an open inquiry). Your inquiry focus must be the same in the document and the presentation.

What you submit

Part Limit Marked by
Inquiry process document (IPD): Inquiry focus then Claims and perspectives, labelled in that order, with a word count for each section 1,500 words in total (references, captions and headings are not counted). Words over the limit are not assessed. Recommended: focus up to 300 words, claims and perspectives up to 1,200. Criterion A (3), Criterion B (6)
Recorded multimedia presentation: Introduction, Analysis and evaluation, Conclusion, labelled in that order, with your own recorded voice throughout 10 minutes. Anything after 10 minutes is not assessed. Criterion C (6), D (6), E (3)
List of references for everything used in the document and presentation No limit Required; citations at the point of use

How the criteria work (in plain words)

Criterion What earns the top band Common weakness
A Inquiry focus (3) An inquiry question with a thorough explanation of how it connects to one specific, relevant real-world example and to course concepts, content and contexts. A vague example ("social media") or missing links to concepts, content or contexts.
B Claims and perspectives (6) A thorough discussion of the claims and perspectives of three sources, each with a clear justification of how it was useful to your inquiry (origin and purpose, meaning and methods, corroboration and use). Summarising sources without discussing their perspective, or fewer than three sources.
C Analysis and evaluation (6) Your own sustained, well-supported analysis and evaluation of impacts and implications for specific people and communities, using evidence. Describing the technology instead of analysing its impacts; claims with no evidence.
D Conclusion (6) Well-supported further insight showing what you now understand, plus a thorough, substantiated discussion of emerging trends and future developments. Repeating earlier points; mentioning the future in one sentence.
E Communication (3) Ideas and evidence organised logically, with media (visuals, text, sound) combined coherently to support understanding; audible voice, legible text. Text-heavy slides read aloud; music drowning out the commentary.

The inquiry stages

1 Focus (weeks 1–3)

  • Choose a real-world example you can investigate in depth: a specific digital system in a specific place affecting specific people (e.g. "algorithmic pay on a food-delivery platform in Nairobi" rather than "the gig economy").

  • Write an inquiry question that starts with "To what extent…", "How…" or "What impacts…" and can be answered in 10 minutes.

  • Map it: which concepts (change, expression, identity, power, space, systems, values and ethics), content (data, algorithms, computers, media, networks, AI, robots) and contexts (cultural, economic, environmental, health, human knowledge, political, social)?

2 Explore (weeks 3–6)

  • Collect a range of sources: news, research, official data, organisations' reports, interviews or first-hand accounts.

  • For each possible source record origin and purpose, meaning and methods, corroboration and use (see the template).

  • Choose the three most useful sources for criterion B; keep every source in your reference list.

3 Investigate (weeks 6–10)

  • Analyse impacts and implications: who benefits, who is harmed, how much, where and why?

  • Use evidence for every claim. Compare perspectives of different stakeholders.

  • Ask: what are the intended and unintended consequences? What trade-offs exist? What would different people say?

4 Reflect (weeks 10–11)

  • What do you now understand that you did not at the start? This is your further insight.

  • Which emerging trends and future developments could change your example (new laws, technologies, business models)? Support them with evidence.

5 Share (weeks 11–14)

  • Write the IPD (1,500 words maximum) and storyboard, record and edit the presentation (10 minutes maximum).

  • Check citations at the point of use (written, visual or spoken) and a full reference list.

  • Your teacher may give feedback on one draft as your school decides; the final work must be your own.

Academic integrity and AI

  • Distinguish clearly between your words and ideas and those of others, in the document and the presentation.

  • If you use AI tools, follow your school's policy and the IB rules: acknowledge their use and never submit generated text as your own analysis.

  • Text-to-speech may be used for the commentary only if the text is your own work.
