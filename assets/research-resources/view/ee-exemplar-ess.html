1. Introduction

Mida Creek, a tidal inlet south of Malindi on the Kenyan coast, is fringed by mangrove forest and is one of the best-known ecotourism sites on the coast. Visitors walk along a raised wooden boardwalk through the mangroves to a hide overlooking the mudflats, where they can watch migratory birds. The boardwalk is managed with the local community, and the income from visitors pays guides and supports conservation work.

Ecotourism is often presented as a way to make conservation pay for itself: if local people earn money from visitors who come to see an ecosystem, they have a reason to protect it. However, visitors also put pressure on the ecosystem they come to see. In a mangrove forest, people who step off a boardwalk to take photographs or look more closely at crabs trample the soft mud, which can crush seedlings and compact the sediment. If this happens often, the forest may regenerate more slowly near the places that visitors use most.

This essay investigates the following research question:

To what extent does distance from the ecotourism boardwalk at Mida Creek, Kenya, affect the density of mangrove (Rhizophora mucronata) seedlings, and what does this suggest about managing visitor access?

The question links an environmental system, the regeneration of the mangrove forest, with a societal system, the ecotourism that depends on it. Answering it requires ecological data on seedling density at different distances from the boardwalk, social data on how visitors behave, and an evaluation of management options from different points of view. It therefore uses the systems approach and the consideration of environmental value systems that are central to ESS. It also has a practical purpose: if visitor behaviour is limiting regeneration, simple and affordable changes to the way the boardwalk is used could protect the forest while keeping the income that gives local people a reason to conserve it.

2. Background

2.1 Mangrove ecosystems and their services

Mangroves are salt-tolerant trees and shrubs that grow in the intertidal zone of tropical coasts. They provide a wide range of ecosystem services: their roots trap sediment and protect the coast from erosion and storm surges; they store large amounts of carbon in their biomass and in waterlogged soils; they act as nurseries for fish and crustaceans that support local fisheries; and they provide wood, honey and tourism income (Rutherford and Williams, 2015). Despite this, mangroves have been lost rapidly in many parts of the world, including East Africa, through cutting for timber and charcoal, conversion to other land uses and pollution.

2.2 How mangroves regenerate

Rhizophora mucronata, one of the most common mangroves at Mida Creek, produces long propagules that begin to germinate while still attached to the parent tree. When they fall, they lodge in the mud and quickly put out roots. For the forest to regenerate, these seedlings must survive their first months, when they are small and vulnerable to being washed away, eaten by crabs or damaged by trampling. Seedling density is therefore a useful indicator of whether a mangrove stand is regenerating successfully. Kairo et al. (2001) describe how natural regeneration in East African mangroves can be limited by disturbance, and how replanting and community-based management have been used to restore degraded areas.

2.3 Ecotourism as a system

The boardwalk at Mida Creek can be understood as part of a social–ecological system. The inputs are visitors, money and labour; the outputs include income for the community, education and enjoyment for visitors, and, potentially, damage to the ecosystem. The mangrove forest is a storage of natural capital that provides the services that attract visitors. Feedback loops link the two systems. If tourism income is used to protect the mangroves, a healthier forest attracts more visitors and more income: positive feedback that strengthens conservation. If visitor pressure damages the forest, the attraction declines, which could eventually reduce visitor numbers: negative feedback that limits tourism but only after damage has been done.

2.4 Environmental value systems

How such a system should be managed depends on people’s values. An ecocentric worldview puts the ecosystem first and favours restricting human activity to protect it. A technocentric worldview trusts technology and management to solve environmental problems, for example by replanting damaged areas. An anthropocentric worldview, which includes many community-based approaches, sees humans as managers who should use the environment sustainably for human benefit (Rutherford and Williams, 2015). Each worldview leads to different management decisions, and an ESS essay needs to consider them rather than assuming a single right answer.

2.5 Trampling as an environmental pressure

Trampling is one of the most widespread local impacts of recreation on natural ecosystems. Feet compress the soil or sediment, reducing the spaces between particles through which water and oxygen move, and directly break or bury small plants. Seedlings are particularly vulnerable because their stems are soft and their roots are shallow. In waterlogged mangrove mud, which is already low in oxygen, compaction can make conditions even harder for young roots. The effect of trampling usually decreases rapidly with distance from a path, because most people stay close to it, which produces a gradient that can be measured with transects running away from the path (Rutherford and Williams, 2015). This is the pattern the investigation looked for.

3. Methodology

3.1 Overall design

The investigation used mixed methods: a quantitative ecological survey to measure seedling density at different distances from the boardwalk, and a questionnaire to find out how visitors behaved and what management options they would support. Using both was essential, because the ecological survey alone could show a pattern but not its cause, while the questionnaire alone could describe visitor behaviour but not its effect on the forest.

3.2 Ecological survey

Six transects were set out at right angles to the boardwalk. Their positions were chosen by generating random numbers for the distance along the boardwalk, excluding the first 20 m from the entrance and the area around the bird hide, where the habitat is different. Along each transect, a 1 m × 1 m quadrat was placed at 0, 5, 10, 15, 20 and 25 m from the edge of the boardwalk. All Rhizophora mucronata seedlings less than 50 cm tall rooted inside the quadrat were counted. Seedlings were recognised by their characteristic propagule and paired leaves. To avoid trampling, the transects were walked along a single line, and the quadrats were placed beside the line rather than on it.

The survey was carried out at low spring tide, when the mud was exposed, on a single day, to reduce differences in conditions. Distance was measured with a 30 m tape (± 0.1 m).

3.3 Questionnaire

Twenty visitors leaving the boardwalk were asked to complete a short, anonymous questionnaire: ten who had taken a guided tour and ten who had walked unguided. The questions asked whether they had stepped off the boardwalk, why, and whether they would support particular management options. Five guides were also asked open questions about visitor behaviour. Before taking part, everyone was told the purpose of the study, that taking part was voluntary, that they could stop at any time, and that no names would be recorded.

3.4 Permission, ethics and safety

Permission was obtained from the site managers before the fieldwork. The survey was planned to minimise disturbance to the habitat: quadrats were placed from the transect line, no plants were removed, and the number of people on the mud was kept to two. Mangrove mud is soft and can be dangerous, so the fieldwork was done with an adult guide who knew the site, at low tide, with the times of the tide checked beforehand.

3.5 Pilot study

Before visiting Mida Creek, I used the VistaEd sampling simulator to practise quadrat sampling and to decide how many quadrats were needed. The simulator showed that the mean density estimated from a small number of quadrats varied widely between runs when the organisms were patchy, and that the estimate became much more stable with six or more quadrats at each position. This led to the decision to use six transects, giving six quadrats at each distance. A short visit to the site with a guide, a week before the survey, confirmed that the mud could be walked on safely at low tide to at least 25 m from the boardwalk on most of its length, and showed where the viewpoints were.

3.6 Analysis plan

Mean seedling density and standard deviation were calculated at each distance. Because counts of seedlings are discrete and not normally distributed, non-parametric tests were chosen: Spearman’s rank correlation to test the relationship between distance and density, and a Kruskal–Wallis test to compare the six distances. A Mann–Whitney U test compared quadrats at 0 m with those at 25 m. For the questionnaire, the proportions of guided and unguided visitors who stepped off the boardwalk were compared with Fisher’s exact test, which is suitable for small samples.

5. Analysis

5.1 Seedling density and distance

Mean seedling density increased steadily with distance from the boardwalk, from 1.7 seedlings m⁻² at the edge of the boardwalk to 9.8 at 10 m and 15.5 at 25 m (Table 1 and Figure 1). The density at 25 m was about nine times the density at the boardwalk. The increase was largest over the first 10 m, and density changed little between 20 and 25 m, which suggests that the effect of the boardwalk extends roughly 20 m into the forest.

Spearman’s rank correlation, using all 36 quadrats, gave rₛ = 0.86 (p < 0.001), a strong and statistically significant positive correlation between distance and seedling density. The Kruskal–Wallis test showed that density differed significantly between the six distances (H = 27.4, df = 5, p < 0.001). A Mann–Whitney U test showed that every quadrat at 25 m had more seedlings than every quadrat at 0 m (U = 0, p = 0.005).

5.2 Variability

The standard deviations increased with distance, from 1.4 seedlings m⁻² at 0 m to 4.5 at 20 m. This is expected for count data: where seedlings are rare, every quadrat has few, but where they are common, local differences in sediment, shading and the positions of parent trees produce larger differences between quadrats. Even so, the ranges at 0 m (0–4) and at 25 m (11–20) did not overlap, so the difference between the two ends of the transects is clear.

5.3 Visitor behaviour

Eight of the twenty visitors (40 %) said they had stepped off the boardwalk at least once (Table 2). The most common reason was to take photographs, followed by getting a closer view of crabs or birds. Two of the ten guided visitors had stepped off, compared with six of the ten unguided visitors. Although this suggests that guiding reduces stepping off, Fisher’s exact test gave p = 0.17, so with this small sample the difference is not statistically significant. All five guides named stepping off the boardwalk as a problem, and two said that it was worst at viewpoints where the boardwalk has no railing.

5.4 Attitudes to management

Most visitors supported measures to protect the mangroves. Sixteen of the twenty (80 %) said they would support a railing at viewpoints, and twelve (60 %) said they would pay KSh 100 more on their entrance fee for a mangrove replanting fund. Support was slightly higher among guided visitors, who may have learned more about the importance of the mangroves during their tour.

5.5 Estimating the size of the effect

To judge whether the effect matters for the forest, its size can be estimated. The mean density between 0 and 20 m from the boardwalk was about 8.4 seedlings m⁻², compared with about 15.5 seedlings m⁻² at 25 m, where the boardwalk seems to have little effect. If 25 m represents the undisturbed density, the zone within 20 m has a deficit of about 7 seedlings in every square metre. Along one side of a 100 m section of boardwalk, this zone has an area of 2,000 m², so roughly 14,000 fewer seedlings would be present than in undisturbed forest. This is a rough estimate based on illustrative data and on the assumption that 25 m represents normal conditions, but it shows that a narrow strip along the boardwalk could contain a large number of missing seedlings. Because only a small fraction of seedlings ever become mature trees, the long-term effect on the forest would be much smaller than this number suggests, but it would be concentrated along the edge that visitors see.

6. Discussion

6.1 Does visitor pressure explain the pattern?

The ecological data show a strong association between distance from the boardwalk and seedling density, and the questionnaire shows that many visitors leave the boardwalk, mostly to take photographs. Together with the footprints and damaged seedlings observed near the boardwalk, this suggests that trampling by visitors is an important reason for the low density of seedlings close to the boardwalk. The shape of the curve also fits this explanation: trampling should be greatest within a few metres of the boardwalk, where visitors step off, and decline with distance as fewer people walk further into the soft mud.

However, the correlation does not prove that visitors are the cause, because other factors also change with distance. The boardwalk itself shades the mud directly beneath and beside it, which could reduce the survival of seedlings that need light. Building the boardwalk may have disturbed the sediment, and the posts may change the way water and sediment move during each tide. The mature trees also differ: some areas close to the boardwalk were cleared when it was built, so there may be fewer parent trees nearby to supply propagules. Each of these could produce lower seedling density near the boardwalk even without trampling.

Two pieces of evidence help to judge the relative importance of these factors. First, the damage to seedlings and the footprints observed near the boardwalk are direct signs of trampling, which shading or sediment changes would not produce. Second, the effect extends about 20 m from the boardwalk, which is much further than its shade would reach. These points suggest that trampling is likely to be a major cause, but the other factors cannot be ruled out without further measurements.

6.2 The system viewpoint

Seen as a system, the results suggest that the boardwalk is causing a small but important negative effect on the natural capital it depends on. The damage is concentrated in a narrow strip along the boardwalk, so it does not threaten the forest as a whole at present. However, if visitor numbers increase, the strip affected could widen, and reduced regeneration could eventually weaken the forest edge that visitors see. This would be an example of negative feedback, where tourism damages the attraction that sustains it. On the other hand, the money from visitors already supports guides and conservation work, and the questionnaire suggests that visitors are willing to pay more to protect the mangroves. Used wisely, tourism income could strengthen the positive feedback between conservation and tourism.

An ecocentric approach would prioritise protecting the regeneration of the forest, for example by installing railings at viewpoints and signs explaining why visitors should stay on the boardwalk. These measures are cheap, the questionnaire shows strong support for them, and they target the places where guides said the problem is worst. A stronger ecocentric approach, closing parts of the boardwalk seasonally, would give maximum protection but would reduce income for the community and might weaken local support for conservation.

A technocentric approach would replant seedlings in the damaged zone. This could restore the area quickly, and Kairo et al. (2001) show that replanting can succeed in East African mangroves. However, if trampling continues, replanted seedlings would also be damaged, so replanting alone treats the symptom rather than the cause.

An anthropocentric, community-based approach would make guided tours compulsory or strongly encouraged. The data suggest, though not significantly, that guided visitors step off less often, and compulsory guiding would increase income for local guides. The risk is that a higher cost would reduce the number of visitors.

Considering all of these, the evidence suggests that a combination is likely to be most effective: railings and signs at viewpoints to reduce trampling where it is worst, encouragement of guided tours, and a small extra charge, which most visitors said they would accept, to fund replanting of the most damaged areas. This combination balances ecocentric protection with the anthropocentric benefits that give the community a reason to conserve the forest, which is the approach that Kairo et al. (2001) recommend for East African mangroves.

6.4 Carrying capacity and sustainability

The results can also be seen in terms of carrying capacity: the number of visitors a site can support without unacceptable damage. At present, the damage appears to be limited to a strip about 20 m wide, and the forest beyond is regenerating well. This suggests that current visitor numbers may be within the carrying capacity of the site, provided the damaged strip does not widen. However, carrying capacity is not fixed: it depends on how visitors behave and how the site is managed. Railings and guiding effectively increase the carrying capacity, because they allow the same number of visitors with less damage. Monitoring seedling density along permanent transects every year would show whether the strip of damage is stable, shrinking or growing, and would give the site managers an early warning if visitor numbers began to exceed what the forest can tolerate. Such monitoring is a practical example of using indicators to judge whether an activity is sustainable.

6.5 The limits of the conclusion

These recommendations depend on the assumption that trampling is the main cause of low seedling density near the boardwalk. If shading or sediment disturbance were more important, railings would make little difference. The recommendations are also based on a single site, a single day of fieldwork and a small, unrepresentative questionnaire sample. They should therefore be seen as reasoned suggestions for the site managers to test, rather than firm conclusions.

7. Evaluation

7.1 Strengths

The mixed-methods design was the main strength of the investigation, because it combined evidence about the state of the ecosystem with evidence about the human behaviour that might affect it. Choosing transect positions randomly reduced the risk of bias in where quadrats were placed. Six transects with six distances each gave 36 quadrats, enough for non-parametric tests, and the use of tests suitable for count data and small samples was appropriate. Considering several management options through different value systems made the evaluation balanced rather than one-sided.

7.2 Weaknesses and improvements

The most important weakness is that the investigation could not separate the effect of trampling from other factors that change with distance. Measuring light intensity at ground level, sediment compaction (with a simple penetrometer) and the density of mature trees in each quadrat would allow the relationship between each factor and seedling density to be tested. Comparing the boardwalk with a control area of similar forest without a boardwalk would also show how much of the pattern is caused by the boardwalk at all.

Second, the survey was carried out on a single day. Seedling numbers change with the seasons, as propagules fall mainly at certain times of year, and visitor numbers vary between holiday and non-holiday periods. Repeating the survey several times over a year would show whether the pattern is consistent and whether it is stronger after busy periods.

Third, the questionnaire sample was small and was not random, because it included only visitors who agreed to take part. Visitors who had stepped off the boardwalk might have been less willing to admit it. A larger sample, perhaps with direct observation of how many visitors step off at each viewpoint, would give more reliable evidence about visitor behaviour, and would make it possible to test the difference between guided and unguided visitors with more statistical power.

Finally, the data in this exemplar are illustrative. A real essay at Mida Creek would require permission from the site managers, and its conclusions would depend on what the student actually found.

7.3 How reliable are the conclusions?

The different parts of the conclusion are supported by evidence of different strength. The pattern itself, lower seedling density close to the boardwalk, is strongly supported: it appeared on all six transects, the differences were much larger than the variation between quadrats, and three different statistical tests agreed. The claim that visitors leave the boardwalk is supported by the questionnaire, the guides and the footprints, although the exact proportion is uncertain because the sample was small. The claim that trampling causes the pattern is the least certain step, because it depends on ruling out other factors that were not measured. The management recommendations depend on all of these steps, so they are the most tentative part of the essay. Recognising this chain of reasoning, and where it is weakest, is important for deciding what the site managers should do next: the most useful next step would be the measurements that test the causal link, before investing in changes.

7.4 Unresolved questions

Would railings at viewpoints actually reduce trampling and increase seedling density over the following year? Do seedlings that survive near the boardwalk grow more slowly? And how do the attitudes of local residents, who depend on the mangroves for fishing and wood, compare with those of visitors?

8. Conclusion

In this investigation, the density of Rhizophora mucronata seedlings at Mida Creek increased strongly with distance from the ecotourism boardwalk, from 1.7 seedlings m⁻² at the edge of the boardwalk to 15.5 at 25 m (rₛ = 0.86, p < 0.001), with most of the change within the first 20 m. Forty per cent of visitors surveyed said they had stepped off the boardwalk, and damaged seedlings and footprints were seen close to it. Distance from the boardwalk is therefore associated with mangrove regeneration to a large extent, and trampling by visitors is the most likely main cause, although shading, sediment disturbance and the distribution of parent trees may also contribute.

The results suggest that managing visitor access could protect regeneration without losing the economic benefits that support conservation. A combination of railings and signs at viewpoints, encouragement of guided tours and a small charge to fund replanting would balance ecocentric and anthropocentric values, and most visitors surveyed said they would support these measures. Because the evidence comes from one site, one day and a small sample, these suggestions should be tested before being adopted, but they show how an ecotourism system can be managed so that tourism and conservation strengthen rather than undermine each other.
