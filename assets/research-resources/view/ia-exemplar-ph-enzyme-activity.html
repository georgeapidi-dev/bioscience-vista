Research question Subject Word count
How does pH (4.0–10.0) affect the initial rate of breakdown of hydrogen peroxide (mmol dm⁻³ min⁻¹) by potato catalase at 25 °C, investigated with a computer simulation of enzyme kinetics? Biology IA (SL/HL) 2,971

Research design

Introduction and personal engagement

In a class practical, I added a cube of raw potato to 3 % hydrogen peroxide and it fizzed vigorously, but when my partner added a few drops of vinegar to the tube the fizzing almost stopped. Our teacher explained that the bubbles were oxygen released by the enzyme catalase, and that the vinegar had changed the pH. I wanted to know whether this was a gradual change or a sudden “switch”, and whether catalase works best at a particular pH. This matters beyond the laboratory: the pH inside cells is kept within narrow limits, and conditions such as acidosis, where blood pH falls below 7.35, can disrupt many enzyme-controlled reactions at once.

Because our school laboratory had a limited supply of buffers and fresh hydrogen peroxide during the term, I first investigated the question with the BioScience Vista enzyme simulator, which models the effect of pH, temperature, substrate concentration and inhibitors on the rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction. Using a model also allowed me to test seven pH values with five repeats each in a single lesson, and to think critically about how far a model can stand in for a real experiment, which I return to in the evaluation.

Background

Enzymes are globular proteins that act as biological catalysts. Each enzyme has an active site whose shape and chemical properties are complementary to its substrate. The substrate binds to the active site through temporary hydrogen bonds and ionic interactions with the R-groups of particular amino acids, forming an enzyme–substrate complex; the reaction then lowers the activation energy and the products are released (Allott and Mindorff, 2014).

Catalase is found in almost all aerobic organisms and is concentrated in peroxisomes. It catalyses the breakdown of hydrogen peroxide, a toxic by-product of metabolism, into water and oxygen: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂. It is one of the fastest enzymes known, so in a test tube the rate is easy to detect as oxygen bubbles or, in the simulator, as product concentration rising over time (Urry et al., 2020).

pH is a measure of hydrogen ion (H⁺) concentration. Many R-groups in proteins can gain or lose H⁺: carboxyl groups (–COOH ⇌ –COO⁻ + H⁺) in aspartate and glutamate, and amino groups (–NH₂ + H⁺ ⇌ –NH₃⁺) in lysine and arginine. When the pH falls, more groups are protonated and negative charges are lost; when the pH rises, H⁺ is removed and positive charges are lost. If these groups are in or near the active site, the change in charge reduces the ionic attraction between enzyme and substrate, so fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form per second. Larger changes alter the ionic and hydrogen bonds that hold the tertiary structure together, changing the shape of the active site; at extreme pH the enzyme is denatured (Urry et al., 2020).

Each enzyme therefore has an optimum pH at which the rate is highest, and the rate falls on either side of it, often giving a bell-shaped curve. The optimum reflects where the enzyme works in the organism: pepsin in the stomach has an optimum near pH 2, while enzymes working in the cytoplasm or peroxisomes usually have optima close to neutral (Allott and Mindorff, 2014). I therefore expected potato catalase to be most active near pH 7.

The rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction is fastest at the start, when the substrate concentration is highest; as the substrate is used up, collisions between substrate and active sites become less frequent and the rate falls. To compare pH values fairly, the rate must therefore be measured at the beginning of the reaction, as the initial rate, before substrate depletion has a noticeable effect. Otherwise a fast reaction at the optimum would appear slower than it really is, because it would have used up more of its substrate by the time it was measured.

The simulator calculates the rate from the Michaelis–Menten equation, with the maximum rate (Vmax) multiplied by a factor for pH that falls away symmetrically on either side of an optimum. For catalase, its “About the model” panel states an optimum of pH 7.0. This is important: the simulation can show the shape that the model predicts, but it cannot by itself prove the optimum of real potato catalase.

Research question

How does pH (4.0, 5.0, 6.0, 7.0, 8.0, 9.0 and 10.0) affect the initial rate of breakdown of hydrogen peroxide (mmol dm⁻³ min⁻¹) by potato catalase at 25 °C, investigated with a computer simulation of enzyme kinetics?

Hypotheses

Null hypothesis (H₀): there is no significant difference in the mean initial rate of reaction between the seven pH values tested.

Alternative hypothesis (H₁): there is a significant difference in the mean initial rate between pH values. The rate will be highest near pH 7.0 and will decrease as the pH moves further from 7.0 in either direction, because the ionisation of R-groups in the active site changes, reducing substrate binding, and at the extremes the enzyme begins to denature.

In a laboratory version, uncontrolled variables such as the age and variety of the potato, and the exact time to mix and start timing, would add variation. In the simulator these are absent, but the model adds a random error of about ±3 % to each run to imitate measurement error, which is why repeats were still needed.

The second pilot was especially useful. At pH 7.0 the product concentration rose almost linearly for about 70 s and then began to curve as substrate was used up. Reading at 60 s therefore gives a value close to the initial rate while still being large enough to read precisely. Reading at 120 s would have underestimated the rate at the optimum more than at other pH values, distorting the shape of the curve.

Methodology

Materials: a laptop with a stable internet connection; the BioScience Vista enzyme activity laboratory (biosciencevista.com/simulations/enzyme/), accessed on 18 November 2026; a spreadsheet for recording.

  1. Opened the enzyme activity laboratory and selected “Catalase (potato)” from the enzyme menu.

  2. Set temperature to 25 °C, substrate concentration to 20 mmol dm⁻³, enzyme concentration to 1.0 and inhibitor to “none”, and checked every setting before each run.

  3. Set the pH slider to 4.0 and pressed Run.

  4. When the timer reached 60 s, read the product concentration from the data table (to 0.01 mmol dm⁻³) and recorded it immediately in the spreadsheet.

  5. Pressed Reset and repeated steps 3–4 until five trials had been recorded at pH 4.0.

  6. Repeated steps 3–5 for pH 5.0, 6.0, 7.0, 8.0, 9.0 and 10.0 (35 runs in total), in a random order generated by the spreadsheet so that any change in the program over time would not follow pH.

  7. Recorded qualitative observations of the animation at each pH, and exported the full data table as a CSV file as a check on the hand-recorded values.

Every reading was recorded; no data were excluded. Because the simulator recalculates the random error each time Run is pressed, the five trials at each pH are independent of one another.

Safety, ethical and environmental considerations

The simulation involves no chemicals, organisms or waste, so there were no safety, ethical or environmental hazards in collecting the data. I took a short screen break after every 15 minutes to limit eye strain. For the planned laboratory comparison, 3 % hydrogen peroxide is an irritant to eyes and skin, so eye protection and gloves would be worn and spills wiped up at once; potato is a food plant, so there are no ethical concerns, and the potato waste would be composted while dilute peroxide and buffers would be poured down the sink with plenty of water.

Data processing

For each pH value I calculated the mean of the five trials as the best estimate of the initial rate. The spread of the trials was described with the sample standard deviation (SD) and with half the range, which I used as the uncertainty of the mean. I also expressed each mean as a percentage of the maximum rate, which makes the effect of pH easier to compare.

Graph

Figure 1: Mean initial rate of hydrogen peroxide breakdown by potato catalase against pH at 25 °C. Error bars show ± 1 SD of five trials; the line joins the means and is not a fitted curve.

The graph shows a clear, symmetrical, bell-shaped relationship. The rate rises steeply from pH 4.0 to 7.0, reaches a single maximum at pH 7.0, and falls equally steeply to pH 10.0. The error bars are so small that they are hidden by the points at the extremes, and no error bars overlap between neighbouring pH values. There are no anomalous results.

The symmetry can be seen by comparing pH values the same distance from the optimum: the rates at pH 6.0 and 8.0 are 79.0 % and 78.7 % of the maximum, those at pH 5.0 and 9.0 are 38.8 % and 39.2 %, and those at pH 4.0 and 10.0 are both 11.3 %. The steepest change is between pH 5.0 and 6.0 and between pH 8.0 and 9.0, where a single pH unit changes the rate by about 40 % of the maximum.

Statistical analysis

Because I was comparing the means of seven independent groups of continuous data, I chose a one-way ANOVA rather than a series of t-tests: carrying out 21 separate t-tests at α = 0.05 would make at least one false positive very likely. Before using ANOVA I checked its assumptions. A Shapiro–Wilk test on each group gave p > 0.05 in every case (p = 0.14 to 0.81), so the data are consistent with a normal distribution, and Levene’s test gave p = 0.10, so the variances can be treated as equal. As a further check I also ran the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test, which does not assume normality.

To find where the optimum lies, I then compared pH 7.0 with its two neighbours using Welch’s t-test, which does not assume equal variances. Because two comparisons were made to test one idea, I used a Bonferroni-corrected significance level of 0.05 ÷ 2 = 0.025. Finally, to test the symmetry of the curve, I compared pH 5.0 with pH 9.0.

The ANOVA result (F(6, 28) = 5818, p < 0.001) means that the probability of differences this large arising by chance, if pH had no effect, is less than 0.1 %, so H₀ is rejected. The Kruskal–Wallis test agrees. The rate at pH 7.0 is significantly higher than at both pH 6.0 and pH 8.0, so within the values tested the optimum is pH 7.0. The rates at pH 5.0 and 9.0 are not significantly different (p = 0.21), which supports the idea that the curve is symmetrical about pH 7.0.

These p-values are extremely small because the simulator’s random error is small. In a real laboratory the SDs would be much larger, so I have also reported the effect size: moving one pH unit from the optimum reduces the rate by about 21 %, two units by about 61 %, and three units by about 89 %.

Conclusion

The results answer the research question clearly. As the pH increased from 4.0 to 7.0, the mean initial rate of hydrogen peroxide breakdown by potato catalase increased from 0.63 ± 0.02 to 5.59 ± 0.11 mmol dm⁻³ min⁻¹, and as the pH increased further to 10.0 the rate decreased to 0.63 ± 0.02 mmol dm⁻³ min⁻¹. The relationship is bell-shaped and symmetrical, with a single optimum at pH 7.0 (to within ± 1 pH unit, the interval tested). The one-way ANOVA (p < 0.001) shows that pH has a statistically significant effect, so the null hypothesis is rejected and the alternative hypothesis is supported, including the prediction that the maximum would be near pH 7.0.

The trend can be explained by the effect of H⁺ concentration on the active site. Below pH 7.0 the higher H⁺ concentration protonates carboxyl groups in the active site, removing negative charges that would normally help to bind and orientate the substrate, so fewer successful enzyme–substrate complexes form each second. Above pH 7.0 the lower H⁺ concentration removes H⁺ from amino groups, losing positive charges, with the same effect. Because the change in charge becomes greater the further the pH moves from the optimum, the rate falls progressively. At pH 4.0 and 10.0, where about 89 % of the activity is lost, the change in charge is likely to have disrupted the ionic bonds that maintain the tertiary structure, so the active site loses its shape and the enzyme is partly denatured, which matches the distorted active site shown in the animation.

An optimum close to neutrality is consistent with the textbook account of catalase working in peroxisomes, where the pH is close to 7 (Allott and Mindorff, 2014), and with the general bell-shaped pH curve of intracellular enzymes (Urry et al., 2020). However, this agreement has to be interpreted carefully. The simulator was programmed with an optimum of pH 7.0 and a symmetrical pH factor, so the data could not have shown any other optimum or shape. My conclusion therefore applies with confidence to the model; for real potato catalase it is a prediction that is supported by the literature but still needs to be tested experimentally.

Evaluation

Reliability of the conclusion

The conclusion that the rate depends strongly on pH, with a maximum near neutrality, is well supported: every mean has a percentage uncertainty below 3 %, neighbouring means are separated by far more than their uncertainties, and both a parametric and a non-parametric test give the same result. The precise position of the optimum is less certain, because it is limited by the 1.0 pH interval, and the shape of the curve is the least certain part of the conclusion, because it is fixed by the model. I am therefore confident about the direction and size of the effect within the model, moderately confident that real potato catalase has an optimum near pH 7, and least confident about the exact symmetry.

Strengths

The method had several strengths. All controlled variables were held exactly constant by the simulator, which is very difficult to achieve in a school laboratory, so the differences between pH values can be attributed to pH alone. The pilot trials justified the range, the reading time and the number of repeats rather than choosing them arbitrarily. Seven pH values gave enough points to show the shape of the curve on both sides of the optimum. Randomising the order of runs and exporting the CSV file as a check reduced the risk of systematic and transcription errors. The percentage uncertainties were all below 3 %, so the data are precise.

Table 5: Evaluation of weaknesses and limitations.

The most important limitation is that the data come from a model rather than from real catalase. The simulator’s assumptions about the optimum and the symmetry of the curve determine the result, so the investigation tests how the model behaves rather than how the enzyme behaves. Real enzymes often show asymmetrical pH curves, because the groups that lose and gain H⁺ on each side of the optimum have different pKa values, and real catalase is known to keep much of its activity over a fairly broad range of pH. A laboratory repeat at the key pH values, using potato extract in phosphate–citrate buffers and measuring oxygen with a gas syringe every 10 s, would test whether the model’s optimum and symmetry are realistic.

The second limitation is the 1.0 pH interval. The statistical tests show that the optimum lies between pH 6.0 and 8.0, but it could be anywhere in that range. Adding intermediate values would narrow it down. Third, the very small random error in the simulator makes the p-values much smaller than they would be with real data. Statistical significance should not be confused with biological importance, which is why I also reported the percentage loss of activity. Finally, reading the product at 60 s slightly underestimates the true initial rate near the optimum, where the curve starts to bend sooner; using the gradient of the first 20–30 s would be more accurate. This is a small systematic error and does not change the shape of the curve or the position of the optimum.

Extensions

The investigation raises two further questions. First, does the optimum pH of catalase depend on its source? Catalase from animal liver could be compared with potato catalase in the laboratory. Second, does temperature shift the pH curve? Repeating the investigation at 37 °C, closer to the optimum temperature in the model, would show whether pH and temperature interact, which could be tested with a two-way ANOVA.
