1. Introduction

Shallow ponds and slow rivers in the tropics are among the most variable environments a plant can live in. On a clear day the water in a shallow pond in Kenya can warm from below 20 °C at dawn to well above 30 °C by mid-afternoon, and in drought years the water level falls and the temperature range becomes even wider. Submerged plants such as Cabomba caroliniana cannot move away from these changes, so the way their photosynthesis responds to temperature helps to decide where they can grow, how fast they produce oxygen for the animals that share the water, and whether they become invasive. Cabomba is a popular aquarium plant that has escaped into waterways on several continents, so its ability to photosynthesise across a wide range of temperatures is also of practical interest.

Most textbook diagrams show photosynthesis rising with temperature to a sharp optimum and then falling rapidly as enzymes denature. These diagrams are based on simple models of enzyme activity. However, research on land plants shows that the temperature response of photosynthesis is not fixed: plants grown in warm conditions can shift their optimum and tolerate higher temperatures (Yamori, Hikosaka and Way, 2014). I wanted to find out whether this is also true for an aquatic plant, and whether a simple model, of the kind used in school simulations, is good enough to explain real data.

This essay therefore investigates the following research question:

To what extent does temperature (5–45 °C) affect the rate of oxygen production by Cabomba caroliniana, and how well does a model of enzyme-controlled photosynthesis explain the results?

The question has two parts. The first asks how large the effect of temperature is, which requires measuring the rate across a wide range and testing the differences statistically. The second asks how well a model explains the results, which requires comparing measured rates with the predictions of the BioScience Vista photosynthesis simulator at the same settings, and explaining any differences with biological theory and published research. Together they allow an argument about how far temperature, on its own, controls photosynthesis in this plant.

2. Background

2.1 The two stages of photosynthesis

Photosynthesis takes place in chloroplasts in two linked stages. In the light-dependent reactions on the thylakoid membranes, light energy absorbed by chlorophyll excites electrons, which pass along an electron transport chain. Water is split (photolysis), releasing oxygen as a by-product, and the energy is used to make ATP and reduced NADP. In the light-independent reactions in the stroma (the Calvin cycle), the enzyme Rubisco fixes carbon dioxide onto ribulose bisphosphate, and ATP and reduced NADP are used to reduce the product to triose phosphate (Allott and Mindorff, 2014). Because oxygen is released by photolysis, the rate of oxygen production is a convenient measure of the overall rate of photosynthesis, provided the other stages keep pace.

2.2 Limiting factors

The rate of photosynthesis is limited by whichever factor is in shortest supply: light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration or temperature. When light is limiting, increasing light increases the rate; when carbon dioxide is limiting, adding hydrogencarbonate to the water increases the rate. Temperature has a different kind of effect, because it changes the rate of the enzyme-controlled reactions of the Calvin cycle and the fluidity of the thylakoid membranes rather than the supply of a raw material (Urry et al., 2020). In this investigation light and carbon dioxide were kept constant so that any change in rate could be attributed to temperature.

2.3 How temperature affects enzyme-controlled reactions

Increasing the temperature increases the kinetic energy of enzyme and substrate molecules, so they collide more often and a greater proportion of collisions have enough energy to react. Many enzyme-controlled reactions roughly double in rate for each 10 °C rise in temperature, a ratio known as the temperature coefficient, Q₁₀. Above an optimum, however, the increased vibration of the enzyme molecules breaks the hydrogen and ionic bonds that hold their tertiary structure, the active site changes shape and the enzyme is denatured. The result is a curve that rises gradually and falls steeply (Allott and Mindorff, 2014).

In photosynthesis the situation is more complicated. Rubisco itself is fairly heat-stable, but the enzyme Rubisco activase, which keeps Rubisco in its active form, is sensitive to heat. At high temperatures the thylakoid membranes also become leaky, so the electron transport chain becomes less efficient. In addition, Rubisco increasingly fixes oxygen instead of carbon dioxide as temperature rises (photorespiration), which wastes energy and reduces net photosynthesis (Urry et al., 2020).

2.4 Temperature acclimation

Yamori, Hikosaka and Way (2014) reviewed how plants respond to growth temperature. They describe two kinds of response: adaptation, which is a genetic difference between species from different climates, and acclimation, which is a change within an individual plant when it grows at a different temperature. In acclimated plants the optimum temperature of photosynthesis moves towards the growth temperature, partly because the plants make more heat-stable forms of Rubisco activase and change the lipid composition of their membranes. This means that two plants of the same species can give different temperature curves if they have been grown in different conditions. A model with fixed parameters cannot show this.

2.5 The simulation model

The BioScience Vista photosynthesis simulator calculates the rate of oxygen production (bubbles per minute) from light intensity, carbon dioxide supply and temperature. Light and carbon dioxide each give a saturating factor, and the rate is set by whichever is smaller, following the principle of limiting factors. Temperature is included as a separate factor that rises towards an optimum of 30 °C and falls more steeply above it. Because this factor is the same for every run, the model represents a plant with a fixed temperature response and no acclimation. Comparing the model with measured data is therefore a way of testing whether acclimation, or other effects not in the model, matter for Cabomba.

3. Methodology

3.1 Choice of method

Three approaches were possible: a laboratory experiment, an analysis of published data, or a simulation. Published data on Cabomba were scarce and were measured under conditions that differed from each other, so comparison would have been unreliable. A simulation alone could only show what the model already assumed. I therefore chose a controlled laboratory experiment as the main source of data, supported by a simulation that was used first to plan the experiment and later as a model to test against the results. Counting bubbles was chosen rather than measuring gas volume because it is quick, allows several repeats at each temperature in one lesson, and is standard in school investigations of photosynthesis. Its limitations are discussed in section 7.

3.2 Pilot study with the simulator

Before collecting any laboratory data, I used the simulator to plan the range of temperatures, check that light and carbon dioxide would not limit the rate, and estimate how many repeats were needed (Table 1).

3.3 Variables

The independent variable was water temperature (5, 15, 25, 30, 35, 40 and 45 °C). Extra values at 30 and 35 °C were included because the pilot suggested the optimum would be in this region. The dependent variable was the rate of oxygen production, measured as the number of bubbles released from the cut end of the stem in one minute. Controlled variables were the light intensity (a 40 W LED lamp at 20 cm, with a heat shield of water between lamp and tube), the carbon dioxide supply (1 % sodium hydrogencarbonate solution), the length of the sprig (8 cm), the time allowed for the plant to adjust to each temperature (5 minutes), and the source of the plants (a single tank kept in the school laboratory).

3.4 Procedure

Sprigs of Cabomba were cut under water to 8 cm and placed upside down in boiling tubes of 1 % sodium hydrogencarbonate. Each tube stood in a water bath, and the temperature inside the tube was checked with a digital thermometer (± 0.1 °C) and kept within ± 0.5 °C of the target by adding hot or cold water. The plant was left for five minutes at each temperature before counting, so that the rate could stabilise. Bubbles were then counted for one minute, five times in succession. A new sprig was used for each temperature so that damage at high temperatures would not affect later readings. The order of temperatures was randomised. At each temperature the same settings were entered into the simulator, and its predicted rate was recorded.

3.5 Analysis plan

Means and standard deviations were calculated for each temperature. A one-way ANOVA was planned to test whether temperature had a significant effect on the rate, followed by a Welch’s t-test to compare the rate at 30 °C with that at 35 °C, to locate the optimum. Q₁₀ values were calculated for the rising part of the curve to compare with the theoretical value of about 2. The measured means were compared with the model predictions both graphically and as differences.

3.6 Safety, ethics and the environment

Hot water above 40 °C can scald, so the water bath was handled with care and the lamp was kept away from water. Cabomba is not an endangered species and no animals were used. Because Cabomba is invasive in some regions, no plant material was released into the environment: used sprigs were returned to the school tank or dried and composted.

5. Analysis

5.1 The effect of temperature

Temperature had a very large effect on the rate of oxygen production. The mean rate rose from 1.8 bubbles min⁻¹ at 5 °C to 10.0 at 15 °C and 28.6 at 25 °C, reached a maximum of 31.0 ± 1.6 bubbles min⁻¹ at 30 °C, and then fell to 28.2 at 35 °C, 19.8 at 40 °C and 8.6 at 45 °C. Between 5 °C and the optimum, the rate increased about seventeen-fold.

A one-way ANOVA showed that these differences are statistically significant (F(6, 28) = 755, p < 0.001). The probability of obtaining differences this large by chance, if temperature had no effect, is negligible. The SDs were small at every temperature (below 1.6 bubbles min⁻¹), which suggests that the counting method was consistent. A Welch’s t-test comparing 30 °C with 35 °C gave t = 3.25 (p = 0.014), so the rate at 30 °C was significantly higher than at 35 °C. The rate at 25 °C (28.6) was also clearly lower than at 30 °C, so the optimum lies between about 25 and 35 °C, most likely near 30 °C.

5.2 The rising part of the curve

The Q₁₀ values in Table 3 help to interpret the rising part of the curve. Between 5 and 15 °C the Q₁₀ was 5.6, and between 15 and 25 °C it was 2.9. Both are higher than the value of about 2 expected for a single enzyme-controlled reaction. One explanation is that at 5 °C more than one process is slowed: the Calvin cycle enzymes, but also the diffusion of carbon dioxide and the movement of molecules within the fluid stroma. At very low temperatures the thylakoid membranes also become less fluid, which slows the electron transport chain. The low rate at 5 °C therefore probably reflects several temperature-sensitive steps acting together. Above 15 °C the Q₁₀ falls towards the expected value, which suggests that a single group of enzyme-controlled reactions becomes the main limit on the rate.

5.3 The falling part of the curve

Above the optimum the rate fell, but more gradually than it rose: it took 15 °C (from 30 to 45 °C) to lose 72 % of the maximum rate. The paler leaves at 45 °C suggest that chlorophyll or the thylakoid membranes were being damaged. The fall can be explained by three processes described in section 2.3: denaturation of heat-sensitive enzymes such as Rubisco activase, leakiness of the thylakoid membranes, and increasing photorespiration. Because oxygen production measures only the light-dependent reactions directly, the fall in oxygen release suggests that, at high temperatures, the electron transport chain itself, or the supply of NADP⁺ and ADP from the Calvin cycle, was being limited.

5.4 Comparison with the model

The comparison with the simulation model (Figure 1 and Table 3) shows a clear pattern. From 5 to 30 °C the model and the laboratory data agree closely: the differences are between −3.9 and +0.9 bubbles min⁻¹, and the model predicts the same optimum of about 30 °C. Above 30 °C they diverge sharply. At 35 °C the plant produced 28.2 bubbles min⁻¹ while the model predicted 20.9; at 40 °C the difference was 15.3 bubbles min⁻¹ (19.8 measured against 4.5 predicted); and at 45 °C the plant still produced 8.6 bubbles min⁻¹ while the model predicted almost none. In other words, the model explains the rising part of the curve well, but predicts a much steeper decline above the optimum than was observed.

The slightly lower measured value at the optimum (31.0 compared with 34.9) is small and could be explained by the model assuming ideal conditions, such as perfect light transmission through the tube and water, that were not achieved in the laboratory.

5.5 Variability and reliability of the data

The SDs give a measure of how consistent the five counts were at each temperature. They were smallest at 25 °C and 40 °C (about 0.5 bubbles min⁻¹) and largest at 30 °C (1.6 bubbles min⁻¹), where the stream of bubbles was fastest and counting was hardest. Expressed as a percentage of the mean, the variation was 7 % or less at every temperature from 15 to 40 °C, rising to 10 % at 45 °C, where fewer bubbles were released. At 5 °C the percentage variation was much larger (about 46 %) because only one to three bubbles were released each minute, so missing or adding a single bubble made a large difference. The rate at 5 °C is therefore the least reliable value in the data set, although its small absolute size means that this does not affect the overall shape of the curve or the conclusion about the optimum.

6. Discussion

6.1 Why does the real plant tolerate heat better than the model?

The most important finding is that Cabomba maintained a considerable rate of photosynthesis above 35 °C, much more than the model predicts. Several explanations are possible.

The first is temperature acclimation. The plants had been kept in a laboratory tank next to a window, where the water often reached 30 °C in the afternoon. According to Yamori, Hikosaka and Way (2014), plants grown at higher temperatures can increase the heat stability of their photosynthetic machinery, for example by producing more heat-stable forms of Rubisco activase and by changing their membrane lipids so that the thylakoids remain less leaky at high temperatures. If the Cabomba plants were acclimated to warm water, their photosynthesis would decline more slowly above the optimum than the model, which represents a plant with a fixed response.

The second explanation is that the plants did not have long enough at each temperature for damage to develop. Heat damage to enzymes and membranes takes time, and five minutes may not have been enough for the full effect to appear. The paler leaves seen after about ten minutes at 45 °C support this idea. If so, the rates measured at high temperatures represent a short-term response and would fall further over a longer exposure. The model, in contrast, behaves as if the full effect of temperature is immediate.

The third explanation concerns the method. At high temperatures the bubbles were noticeably smaller. Because bubble counting assumes that each bubble contains the same volume of gas, smaller bubbles would make the rate appear higher than it is if the volume of oxygen is considered. In addition, warm water holds less dissolved gas, so oxygen may come out of solution more easily, and some bubbles at high temperatures may have contained gases other than oxygen released from the water. These effects would overestimate the rate at high temperatures and so exaggerate the difference between the plant and the model.

These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and the data cannot distinguish between them. However, the size of the difference at 40 °C, more than four times the predicted rate, seems too large to be explained by bubble size alone, which suggests that a real biological difference, such as acclimation, is part of the explanation.

6.2 How well does the model explain the results?

The model is successful in two ways. It correctly predicts the optimum of about 30 °C and the shape of the rising part of the curve, which supports the idea that this part is controlled mainly by the effect of temperature on enzyme-controlled reactions. It is less successful above the optimum, where it assumes a rapid and fixed decline. This reflects a general limitation of simple models: they represent the average behaviour of an organism under particular assumptions, and they cannot show responses, such as acclimation, that depend on the history of the organism. The model is therefore useful for predicting the general trend and for planning an investigation, as it was used here, but it should not be used to predict the exact rates of a particular plant at high temperatures.

6.3 Links to the wider context

If Cabomba can maintain photosynthesis at temperatures where the model predicts very little, it may be better able to survive in warming waters than simple predictions suggest. This would help to explain its success as an invasive species in warm regions. It also has implications for oxygen levels in ponds: if submerged plants continue to release oxygen during hot afternoons, they may partly offset the lower solubility of oxygen in warm water, which is important for fish and invertebrates. These suggestions go beyond the data, but they show why the shape of the response above the optimum matters ecologically.

6.4 How strong is the evidence?

The different parts of the answer to the research question are supported by evidence of different strength. The claim that temperature has a large and significant effect on the rate is strongly supported: the differences between temperatures are many times larger than the variation between repeats, and the ANOVA result is highly significant. The claim that the optimum lies near 30 °C is moderately strong, because it is supported by a significant difference between 30 and 35 °C but limited by the 5 °C interval around the peak. The claim that the model fails above the optimum is strong in direction, because the measured rates exceed the predicted rates at every temperature above 30 °C, but weaker in size, because bubble counting may have overestimated the rates at high temperatures. Finally, the explanation that acclimation causes the difference is the weakest part of the argument: it is consistent with the conditions in which the plants were grown and with published research, but it was not tested directly. A careful answer to the research question should therefore distinguish between what the data show and what they suggest.

7. Evaluation

7.1 Strengths

The investigation had several strengths. The pilot study with the simulator made it possible to choose a sensible range, check that light and carbon dioxide were not limiting, and add extra temperatures near the expected optimum. Temperature was measured inside the tube rather than in the water bath, and kept within ± 0.5 °C. Using a new sprig for each temperature, and randomising the order, prevented damage at one temperature from affecting the next. Five repeats at each temperature gave small SDs, and both an overall test (ANOVA) and a focused test (Welch’s t-test) were used to support the conclusions. Comparing the data with a model added a second line of evidence and made it possible to discuss why real plants may differ from simple predictions.

7.2 Limitations

The main limitation is the use of bubble counts as a measure of the rate. Bubbles varied in size, especially at high temperatures, and their composition was not known. This makes the rate an index rather than a true volume of oxygen, and it may have overestimated the rate above 35 °C. Using a photosynthometer to collect the gas and measure its volume in a capillary tube, or a dissolved oxygen probe, would give more valid data.

A second limitation is the short adjustment time of five minutes. As discussed in section 6.1, the full effect of high temperatures may take longer to appear. Measuring the rate at intervals over 30 minutes at each high temperature would show whether the rate continues to fall.

Third, the acclimation explanation was not tested directly. All the plants came from one tank kept at warm temperatures, so the results describe one population in one set of conditions. Growing two groups of Cabomba at different temperatures (for example 20 °C and 30 °C) for several weeks and then measuring their temperature responses would test whether acclimation explains the difference from the model.

Fourth, the light from the lamp may have warmed the tube slightly despite the heat shield, and it was difficult to keep the temperature exactly constant at 45 °C. Using a thermostatically controlled water bath would reduce this source of error.

Finally, the data presented in this exemplar are illustrative. In a real essay these values would be replaced by the student’s own measurements, and the analysis and discussion would depend on what the student actually found.

7.3 Unresolved questions

The investigation raises questions that could be explored further. Does the optimum temperature of Cabomba depend on the temperature at which it was grown? Does exposure time change the shape of the curve above the optimum? And do other submerged plants, such as Elodea, show the same tolerance of high temperatures?

8. Conclusion

Temperature has a large effect on the rate of oxygen production by Cabomba caroliniana. In this investigation the rate increased about seventeen-fold from 5 °C to an optimum near 30 °C, and then fell, losing about 72 % of the maximum rate by 45 °C. The differences were statistically significant. The rising part of the curve, with Q₁₀ values falling from 5.6 to 2.9, is consistent with the effect of temperature on enzyme-controlled reactions and on membrane fluidity.

A simple model of enzyme-controlled photosynthesis explains the results to a large extent below the optimum, predicting both the shape of the curve and the optimum temperature. Above the optimum it explains them only partly: the plant maintained a much higher rate than predicted at 35–45 °C. The most likely explanations are temperature acclimation of the plants to their warm growing conditions and the short time allowed at each temperature, with some overestimation of the rate by bubble counting. Temperature is therefore a major factor controlling photosynthesis in Cabomba, but the response above the optimum depends on the plant’s history and on how long it is exposed, which a fixed model cannot capture.
