1. Introduction

Before a squash match, players hit the ball hard against the walls for several minutes. A cold squash ball hardly bounces at all, and the game is slow and difficult; after the warm-up the ball becomes lively and fast. Squash balls are even graded by how much they bounce: the double-yellow-dot ball used by experienced players has the lowest bounce and needs to be warmed most before it plays properly.

This everyday observation raises a physics question. A bounce is a collision in which some of the ball’s kinetic energy is returned and some is lost, and the fraction returned is described by the coefficient of restitution. Why should this depend so strongly on temperature, and is the relationship simple or complicated? Two physical explanations are possible: the rubber itself may lose less energy when warm, or the air inside the hollow ball may push back harder at higher temperatures. Measuring how the coefficient of restitution changes with temperature, and comparing the trend with these explanations, is the aim of this essay.

The research question is:

How does the temperature of a squash ball (10–60 °C) affect its coefficient of restitution when dropped from 1.00 m onto a concrete floor?

Answering this question requires precise measurement of rebound heights at controlled temperatures, calculation of the coefficient of restitution with its uncertainty, and analysis of the shape of the relationship. The discussion then uses the physics of rubber and of gases to explain the results and to judge which effect is more important.

2. Background

2.1 The coefficient of restitution

When a ball hits a hard surface, the coefficient of restitution, e, is the ratio of the speed of separation to the speed of approach. For a ball bouncing on a fixed floor, e = v₂ ÷ v₁, where v₁ is the speed just before impact and v₂ is the speed just after. A perfectly elastic ball would have e = 1 and bounce back to its original height; a ball that did not bounce at all would have e = 0 (Cross, 2000).

If air resistance is negligible, a ball dropped from rest from height h₁ hits the floor with a speed given by energy conservation: ½mv₁² = mgh₁, so v₁ = √(2gh₁). Similarly, a ball that leaves the floor at speed v₂ rises to height h₂ where v₂ = √(2gh₂). Therefore:

e = √(h₂ ÷ h₁)

This allows e to be found simply by measuring the drop height and the rebound height. The fraction of kinetic energy lost in each bounce is 1 − e², because kinetic energy is proportional to the square of speed.

2.2 Where does the energy go?

During a bounce the ball is squashed against the floor and then springs back. While it is compressed, its kinetic energy is stored as elastic potential energy in the rubber and in the compressed air inside. If the rubber were perfectly elastic, all this energy would be returned. In real rubber, however, the long polymer chains rub against each other as they are stretched and released, converting some of the energy into internal energy (heat). This effect, called hysteresis, means that the force during expansion is smaller than during compression, and the difference in work done is the energy lost (Cross, 2000). Some energy is also lost as sound and as vibrations of the ball and the floor.

2.3 How temperature affects rubber

Rubber consists of long, tangled polymer chains joined by occasional cross-links. Its elasticity comes mainly from the tendency of the chains to return to a disordered, coiled arrangement after being stretched, which is an entropy effect (Treloar, 2005). At low temperatures, the chains move sluggishly and the internal friction between them is high, so a large fraction of the energy is lost in each deformation. As the temperature rises, the chains become more mobile, they can respond quickly to the rapid deformation of a bounce, and less energy is dissipated. Near and below a certain temperature, the glass transition temperature, rubber becomes stiff and leathery and bounces very poorly. Squash balls are made from rubber compounds designed to have relatively high energy loss at room temperature, so that the ball is slow, which is why warming makes such a large difference.

2.4 How temperature affects the air inside

A squash ball is hollow and sealed. When it is warmed, the pressure of the air inside rises. For a fixed volume of gas, the ideal gas law gives p ∝ T, where T is the absolute temperature. Heating the ball from 10 °C (283 K) to 60 °C (333 K) would therefore increase the internal pressure by a factor of 333 ÷ 283 = 1.18, an increase of 18 %. A higher internal pressure makes the ball stiffer and helps it to push back against the floor, which would also increase e.

2.5 Assumptions of the method

The equation e = √(h₂ ÷ h₁) assumes that air resistance is negligible and that the floor does not move. A squash ball has a mass of about 24 g and a diameter of about 40 mm. Falling 1 m, it reaches a speed of about 4.4 m s⁻¹, at which the drag force is only a few per cent of its weight, and on the shorter rebound the speed is even lower. Air resistance would therefore reduce h₂ very slightly and make e appear slightly smaller than its true value, by a similar fraction at every temperature, so it would not change the trend. A concrete floor is very much stiffer and more massive than the ball, so it absorbs very little energy and can be treated as fixed. Spin would also change the bounce, because some energy would go into rotation, which is why the ball was released carefully without spin.

2.6 Predictions

Both effects predict that e will increase with temperature. They differ in the shape of the relationship. The gas-pressure effect increases linearly with absolute temperature. The rubber effect is not linear: the energy loss changes most rapidly in a particular temperature range and then levels off as the rubber approaches its most elastic state. If the rubber effect dominates, e should rise steeply at first and then more slowly at higher temperatures.

3. Methodology

3.1 Choice of method

Rebound heights can be measured by eye against a metre rule, but this is imprecise because the ball is at its highest point for only a fraction of a second, and parallax errors are large. Instead, each bounce was filmed with a smartphone at 240 frames per second, and the rebound height was measured frame by frame in the free video analysis program Tracker. This made it possible to find the highest point of the bounce precisely and to check that the ball was dropped from rest. Measuring heights rather than speeds was chosen because heights can be measured with a smaller percentage uncertainty in a school laboratory.

3.2 Variables

The independent variable was the temperature of the ball: 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 °C. The range was chosen to include temperatures below room temperature, which a cold ball might reach on a winter court, and temperatures up to 60 °C, which is higher than a ball normally reaches in play but safe to handle. The dependent variable was the coefficient of restitution, calculated from the drop height and the rebound height. The controlled variables were the ball (a single double-yellow-dot squash ball, used for all trials), the drop height (1.000 ± 0.002 m, measured to the bottom of the ball), the surface (the same tile of a concrete floor), the time between removing the ball from the water bath and dropping it (5 s), and the release method (held lightly between the fingertips and released without spin).

3.3 Procedure

A metre rule was fixed vertically beside the drop point, and the phone was mounted on a tripod 1.5 m away, level with the expected rebound height, to reduce parallax. The ball was placed in a sealed plastic bag in a water bath for 10 minutes at the chosen temperature, measured with a digital thermometer (± 0.1 °C) and held within ± 0.5 °C. The bag kept the ball dry. The ball was removed, held at the drop height and released within 5 s, while the phone filmed the bounce. Three drops were made at each temperature, returning the ball to the bath for 3 minutes between drops. The temperatures were tested in a random order.

3.4 Trial run

Before the main experiment, a short trial was carried out at room temperature. It showed three problems that changed the method. First, when the rebound height was judged by eye, three observers disagreed by up to 2 cm, which confirmed the need for video analysis. Second, filming at the normal 30 frames per second sometimes missed the highest point of the bounce, so 240 frames per second was used. Third, a ball taken from the water without a bag left a wet patch on the floor, which changed the bounce of the next drop, so the ball was kept in a sealed bag and dried before each drop.

3.5 Analysis plan

For each video, the rebound height h₂ was measured in Tracker by finding the frame where the ball was highest. e was calculated as √(h₂ ÷ h₁). The mean and standard deviation of e were calculated for each temperature, and the uncertainty in each value of e was found by propagating the uncertainties in h₁ and h₂. The data were plotted with error bars, and the relationship was tested with Spearman’s rank correlation and by fitting linear and quadratic models.

3.6 Safety and environment

Water at 60 °C can scald, so the ball was handled with tongs, and the water bath was placed on a stable bench away from the drop area. The floor was kept dry. The water was reused between sessions, and the plastic bags were reused.

5. Analysis

5.1 The overall trend

The coefficient of restitution increased with temperature at every step, from 0.236 ± 0.001 at 10 °C to 0.458 ± 0.009 at 60 °C. In other words, the speed with which the ball left the floor almost doubled over the range studied. The fraction of kinetic energy lost in each bounce fell from 94.4 % at 10 °C to 79.0 % at 60 °C. Even when warm, therefore, a squash ball loses most of its energy in each bounce, which is consistent with its design as a slow, low-bounce ball.

Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient for all 18 measurements was rₛ = 0.99 (p < 0.001), showing a very strong, significant positive relationship between temperature and the coefficient of restitution.

5.2 Is the relationship linear?

A linear fit to all 18 points gave a gradient of 4.53 × 10⁻³ °C⁻¹ (± 0.15 × 10⁻³ °C⁻¹, standard error) and r² = 0.982. Although r² is high, the residuals show a systematic pattern: the measured values are below the line at 10 °C (by 0.009) and at 60 °C (by 0.013), and above it in the middle of the range (by up to 0.014 at 40 °C). Systematic residuals of this kind mean that a straight line is not the best description of the data.

The increases in e between successive temperatures also show the curvature. From 10 to 20 °C, e increased by 0.055; from 20 to 30 °C by 0.046; from 30 to 40 °C by 0.057; from 40 to 50 °C by 0.036; and from 50 to 60 °C by 0.028. Apart from the 30–40 °C interval, the increases become steadily smaller, so the curve flattens at higher temperatures. A quadratic model, e = −3.1 × 10⁻⁵ T² + 6.7 × 10⁻³ T + 0.170, fits the data better, and its negative T² term confirms the decreasing rate of increase.

5.3 Uncertainties

The uncertainty in e due to the measurement of heights was between ± 0.006 and ± 0.011 (Table 2). It was largest at low temperatures, because the rebound heights were smallest there (about 5.6 cm at 10 °C), so a ± 0.005 m uncertainty in h₂ was a large percentage of the measurement. At 10 °C, for example, Δh₂ ÷ h₂ = 0.005 ÷ 0.056 = 9 %, giving an uncertainty in e of about 4.5 %. The standard deviations of the three drops were between 0.001 and 0.011, similar in size to the propagated uncertainties. This suggests that the main source of random variation was the measurement of rebound height rather than differences between drops. In all cases, the uncertainty was much smaller than the change in e across the range, so the trend is clearly real.

5.4 Energy analysis

Expressing the results as energies makes their physical meaning clearer. For a ball of mass about 24 g dropped from 1.00 m, the kinetic energy just before impact is mgh₁ ≈ 0.024 × 9.81 × 1.00 ≈ 0.24 J. At 10 °C only 5.6 % of this, about 0.013 J, is returned; at 60 °C, 21.0 %, about 0.050 J, is returned. In other words, warming the ball from 10 to 60 °C almost quadruples the energy it gets back from each bounce, even though most of the energy is still lost. Because the energy returned is proportional to e², small increases in e correspond to large increases in returned energy, which is why players notice the effect of temperature so strongly.

6. Discussion

6.1 Explaining the increase in e

The increase in e with temperature is consistent with both mechanisms described in section 2. As the rubber warms, its polymer chains become more mobile and the energy dissipated by hysteresis during each bounce decreases, so a larger fraction of the stored energy is returned as kinetic energy. At the same time, the pressure of the air inside the ball increases, making the ball stiffer and helping it to rebound. The question is how much each mechanism contributes.

6.2 Which effect is more important?

The gas effect can be estimated. Between 10 and 60 °C the internal pressure rises by about 18 %. Even if this increased the stored energy returned by the same percentage, it could only account for an increase in e of roughly 9 %, because e depends on the square root of the energy returned. The observed increase in e, from 0.236 to 0.458, is about 94 %, which is ten times larger. The gas effect also predicts a linear relationship with temperature, whereas the data show a curve that flattens at higher temperatures.

The rubber effect, on the other hand, can explain both the size and the shape of the change. As Treloar (2005) describes, the mechanical properties of rubber change most rapidly over a particular range of temperature, as the material moves away from its glassy, high-loss state towards its fully elastic, low-loss state. The steep rise at low temperatures and the levelling off at higher temperatures are what would be expected if the squash ball rubber were moving through this transition. The data therefore suggest that the main reason a squash ball bounces better when warm is the change in the properties of the rubber, with the increased air pressure making a smaller, secondary contribution.

6.3 An alternative explanation for the flattening

There is, however, an experimental explanation for the flattening that must be considered. During the 5 s between removing the ball from the water bath and dropping it, the ball begins to cool towards room temperature (about 24 °C). By Newton’s law of cooling, the rate of heat loss is proportional to the difference between the ball’s temperature and its surroundings, so the hottest balls cool fastest. A ball removed at 60 °C would cool more in 5 s than one removed at 30 °C, so the true temperature of the ball at impact would be further below the nominal temperature at the top of the range. This would make the measured values of e at high temperatures appear lower than they should for that nominal temperature, producing a flattening even if the true relationship were linear.

The same effect works in the opposite direction at 10 °C, where the ball would warm slightly towards room temperature before the drop. Both effects compress the range of true temperatures compared with the nominal ones, which could explain part of the curvature. Without measuring the temperature of the ball at the moment of impact, the data cannot fully separate this experimental effect from the real behaviour of the rubber. However, a 5 s exposure is short compared with the time needed to cool a rubber shell significantly, so it is unlikely to explain the whole effect.

6.4 Comparison with published work

Cross (2000) measured the coefficients of restitution of several types of ball and showed that the energy loss depends strongly on the material: balls made of high-hysteresis rubber can lose most of their energy in a single bounce. The very low values of e found here, especially at low temperatures, fit this description of a high-loss material. Squash ball manufacturers use exactly this property to produce balls of different speeds, and the need to warm a double-yellow-dot ball before play is consistent with the large increase in e measured in this investigation.

6.5 Implications for squash

In squash, a higher coefficient of restitution means the ball returns from the walls and floor faster, giving players less time to reach it. The results suggest that a ball at 40 °C, a temperature commonly reached during play, would leave the floor about 67 % faster from the floor than a ball at 10 °C. This explains why a warm-up period is part of the game, and why matches in cold courts are slower and longer.

6.6 Limitations of the explanation

The explanation given above treats the bounce as a single process with a fixed energy loss. In reality the bounce lasts only a few milliseconds, and the rubber is deformed very quickly. The energy lost by hysteresis depends not only on temperature but also on how fast the rubber is deformed, because the polymer chains need time to rearrange. A faster impact, for example from a greater drop height or a hard shot in a game, deforms the rubber more quickly, and the ball may behave as if it were colder. This means that the values of e measured in a 1 m drop may not be the same as those during a match, where the ball hits the wall at speeds of more than 40 m s⁻¹. The trend with temperature is likely to be similar, but the actual values would differ. Testing different drop heights at each temperature would show whether the effect of temperature depends on the speed of impact, and would connect the laboratory results more closely to the game.

7. Evaluation

7.1 Strengths

Using high-speed video and Tracker allowed the rebound height to be found far more precisely than by eye, and it also confirmed that each ball was released from rest without spin. Using a single ball removed differences between balls. The water bath allowed the ball’s temperature to be set precisely and kept the ball dry inside a bag. Randomising the order of temperatures prevented any gradual change in the ball, such as wear, from producing a false trend. Propagating uncertainties and analysing residuals made it possible to judge whether the curvature was real.

7.2 Weaknesses and improvements

The most important weakness was the uncertainty in the actual temperature of the ball at impact, discussed in section 6.3. This systematic error grows with the difference between the ball’s temperature and room temperature, so it could distort the shape of the relationship. Measuring the surface temperature of the ball with an infrared thermometer immediately before release, or warming the ball in an insulated chamber at the drop height so that it could be dropped without being moved, would reduce this error.

A second weakness was that only one ball was used. Squash balls of the same type can differ slightly, so repeating the experiment with several balls would show whether the results are typical. Using a different type of ball, such as a single-blue-dot ball, would show whether the shape of the curve depends on the rubber compound.

Third, the separation of the rubber effect from the gas effect relied on calculation rather than measurement. Testing a solid rubber ball, which has no internal air, would show how much of the change is due to the rubber alone. Alternatively, a small hole could be made in a squash ball so that the internal pressure could not change.

Fourth, only three drops were made at each temperature. Five drops would give more reliable means and a better estimate of the random uncertainty, especially at low temperatures where the percentage uncertainty in h₂ was greatest. Dropping from a greater height, such as 2.00 m, would also increase h₂ and reduce its percentage uncertainty.

Finally, the data in this exemplar are illustrative. In a real essay, the results and the strength of the evidence would depend on the student’s own measurements.

7.3 Unresolved questions

At what temperature does the coefficient of restitution of a squash ball stop increasing, and does it eventually decrease at temperatures above 60 °C? And does the surface the ball hits, such as wood or plaster, which also absorb energy, change the temperature dependence?

8. Conclusion

The temperature of a squash ball has a large effect on its coefficient of restitution. Over the range 10–60 °C, e increased from 0.236 to 0.458, almost doubling, and the fraction of kinetic energy lost per bounce fell from 94 % to 79 %. The relationship was strongly positive (rₛ = 0.99, p < 0.001) but not linear: e increased steeply at low temperatures and more slowly at higher temperatures, and a quadratic model described the data better than a straight line.

The size and shape of the change are best explained by the effect of temperature on the rubber: as the rubber warms, its polymer chains become more mobile and less energy is lost through hysteresis. The increase in the pressure of the air inside the ball contributes, but calculation suggests that it accounts for only a small part of the change. Some of the flattening at high temperatures may be due to the ball cooling before it was dropped, so the exact shape of the curve should be treated with caution. Overall, warming a squash ball makes it bounce much better, mainly because its rubber becomes more elastic.
