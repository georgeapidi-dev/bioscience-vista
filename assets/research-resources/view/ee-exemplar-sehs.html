1. Introduction

Coaches and athletes look for simple ways to measure fitness without expensive laboratory equipment. One of the simplest is heart-rate recovery: how quickly the heart rate falls after exercise stops. A chest-strap monitor or even a stopwatch and a finger on the wrist are enough to measure it, and many sports watches now report it automatically. It is widely believed that fitter people recover faster, and heart-rate recovery is used both to monitor training and, in medicine, as an indicator of cardiovascular health.

In my school, students who train regularly for football or athletics often seem to recover from a hard run much faster than those who do little exercise. But is this difference large and reliable enough to be useful, and is it really caused by training, or by other differences between the two groups, such as the fact that the same exercise is easier for a fitter person? These questions matter because if heart-rate recovery is to be used to judge fitness, its results must be interpreted correctly.

This essay investigates the research question:

To what extent does training status (trained vs untrained 16–18-year-olds) affect heart-rate recovery in the five minutes after a 2-minute step test?

Answering this question requires measuring heart rate during recovery in two groups under a standard protocol, comparing the rate of recovery statistically, and explaining the differences with the physiology of the cardiovascular and autonomic nervous systems. The discussion also considers how far the differences can be attributed to training rather than to the way the test was designed.

2. Background

2.1 Control of heart rate

The heart has its own pacemaker, the sinoatrial node, which would make it beat at about 100 beats per minute (bpm) if it were not influenced by the nervous system. At rest, the parasympathetic nervous system, acting through the vagus nerve, slows the heart to a typical resting rate of 60–80 bpm. During exercise, two changes increase the heart rate: parasympathetic (vagal) activity is withdrawn, and sympathetic activity and circulating adrenaline increase. Together these raise heart rate and the force of contraction, increasing cardiac output to deliver more oxygen to working muscles (Kenney, Wilmore and Costill, 2015).

2.2 What happens when exercise stops

When exercise stops, the heart rate does not return to its resting value immediately. It falls in two phases. In the first minute or so, it falls quickly, mainly because vagal activity returns rapidly. After this, the fall is slower and depends on the gradual decrease in sympathetic activity and adrenaline, the removal of metabolites from the muscles, and the cooling of the body. Heart-rate recovery is therefore usually measured as the fall in heart rate in the first minute (HRR1) or the first two minutes (HRR2) after exercise (Daanen et al., 2012).

2.3 How training affects the heart

Regular endurance training causes several adaptations. The left ventricle becomes larger and its walls stronger, so stroke volume, the volume of blood pumped in each beat, increases. Because more blood is pumped per beat, the same cardiac output can be achieved with a lower heart rate, so trained people have lower resting heart rates. Training also increases vagal tone, the level of parasympathetic activity, which contributes further to the low resting heart rate and to the rapid fall in heart rate after exercise (Kenney, Wilmore and Costill, 2015).

2.4 Heart-rate recovery as a measure of training status

Daanen et al. (2012) reviewed studies that used heart-rate recovery to monitor athletes. They concluded that heart-rate recovery is sensitive to changes in training status, improving as athletes become fitter, but that it is strongly affected by the conditions of measurement. In particular, the intensity of the preceding exercise matters: after harder exercise, heart rate takes longer to recover. They recommended standardising the exercise and the conditions carefully, and they cautioned that comparisons between individuals are more difficult than tracking changes in the same person.

2.5 Measuring heart rate

Heart rate can be measured by palpation, feeling the pulse at the radial artery in the wrist or the carotid artery in the neck and counting beats for a fixed time, or with electronic monitors. Palpation is cheap, but counting for 15 seconds and multiplying by four means that missing or adding a single beat changes the result by 4 bpm, and during recovery the heart rate changes so quickly that a 15-second count averages over a changing value. Optical sensors in watches can be affected by movement and by how tightly they are worn. Chest-strap monitors detect the electrical activity of the heart directly and are generally the most accurate practical method outside a laboratory, which is why they are often used in research on heart-rate recovery (Daanen et al., 2012).

2.6 Predictions

From this theory, trained participants should have a lower heart rate at the end of a standard exercise test, and a larger and faster fall in heart rate during recovery, particularly in the first minute, when vagal reactivation dominates. However, because the same absolute exercise is relatively easier for trained participants, part of any difference might be due to the lower relative intensity rather than to faster recovery itself. This possibility must be considered when interpreting the results.

3. Methodology

3.1 Choice of method

A step test was chosen because it is safe, needs little equipment, can be standardised precisely with a metronome, and is widely used in schools and research. A 2-minute test is long enough to raise heart rate substantially but short enough to be safe for untrained participants. Heart rate was measured with chest-strap monitors rather than by palpation, because they are more precise (± 1 bpm) and can record continuously, which avoids the problem of counting the pulse while the heart rate is changing quickly.

3.2 Pilot with the pulse simulator

Before recruiting participants, I used the VistaEd pulse simulator to plan the protocol. The simulator models the recovery of heart rate after exercise in a fitter and a less fit volunteer. It showed that most of the difference between them appears in the first two minutes, and that heart rate is close to resting values after about five minutes in a fitter person. This suggested that recording every minute for five minutes would capture the important part of the recovery curve. The simulator also showed that counting the radial pulse for 15 seconds introduces errors of several beats per minute, which supported the use of chest-strap monitors.

3.3 Participants

Twenty students aged 16–18 took part (Table 1). Ten were classified as trained, meaning they trained for at least four sessions a week in football, athletics or swimming and had done so for at least six months. Ten were classified as untrained, meaning they did no more than one session of exercise a week outside physical education lessons. Participants were recruited by invitation through the school’s sports department and form tutors.

3.4 Ethics and safety

Every participant completed the Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire (PAR-Q), and anyone who answered “yes” to any question would have been excluded; nobody was. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant and, because they were under 18, from a parent or guardian. Participants were told that they could withdraw at any time without giving a reason, and that their results would be kept anonymous and identified only by a code. A teacher trained in first aid was present during all testing. The test was stopped immediately if a participant felt unwell; this did not happen. Results were stored without names and deleted after the essay was completed.

3.5 Protocol

All tests were carried out in the school sports hall between 10:00 and 12:00, to reduce differences caused by time of day, and participants were asked not to exercise strenuously, drink caffeine or eat a large meal in the two hours before the test. Each participant sat quietly for five minutes while wearing the chest-strap monitor, and the lowest heart rate in the final minute was recorded as the resting heart rate. They then stepped onto and off a 30 cm step at 30 steps per minute, kept in time by a metronome, for exactly two minutes. As soon as they stopped, they sat down, and their heart rate was recorded at the end of exercise (0 minutes) and at the end of each of the following five minutes.

3.6 Variables

The independent variable was training status (trained or untrained). The dependent variable was heart rate during recovery, from which heart-rate recovery at one minute (HRR1 = heart rate at 0 min − heart rate at 1 min) and two minutes (HRR2 = heart rate at 0 min − heart rate at 2 min) were calculated. Controlled variables included step height, stepping rate, duration of exercise, recovery position (seated), time of day, room temperature (22–24 °C), and the restrictions on caffeine, food and exercise before testing.

3.7 Analysis plan

Mean heart rates and standard deviations were calculated for each group at each time. HRR1 and HRR2 were calculated for every participant. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to check whether the data were normally distributed. The groups were compared with Welch’s t-test, which does not assume equal variances, and the result for HRR1 was checked with the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test. Cohen’s d was calculated to show the size of the difference.

5. Analysis

5.1 Heart rate at the end of exercise

At the end of the step test, the trained group’s mean heart rate was 146 ± 4 bpm, compared with 161 ± 6 bpm in the untrained group, a difference of 15 bpm that was statistically significant (Welch’s t = 6.96, p < 0.001). The same exercise therefore raised the heart rate of untrained participants considerably more. This is consistent with the larger stroke volume of trained participants: to deliver the same cardiac output to working muscles, they needed fewer beats per minute.

5.2 The shape of the recovery curves

Both groups showed the expected two-phase recovery (Figure 1). Heart rate fell steeply in the first minute and then more slowly. In the trained group, heart rate fell from 146 to 91 bpm in the first minute and to 73 bpm after two minutes, and by five minutes it was 60 bpm, the same as the group’s resting heart rate within the variation between participants. In the untrained group, heart rate fell from 161 to 125 bpm in the first minute and 106 bpm after two minutes, and was still 83 bpm after five minutes, above the group’s resting value of 77 bpm. The difference between the groups was largest at one and two minutes (34 bpm) and became smaller later in recovery (22 bpm at five minutes).

5.3 Heart-rate recovery

In the first minute, heart rate fell by 54 ± 6 bpm in trained participants and by 36 ± 9 bpm in untrained participants (Table 3). The Shapiro–Wilk test showed no evidence that HRR1 departed from a normal distribution in either group (p = 0.86 and 0.95), so a t-test was appropriate. Welch’s t-test gave t = 5.15 (df = 15.4, p < 0.001), and the Mann–Whitney U test agreed (U = 96.5, p < 0.001). The difference in HRR2 was similar: 73 ± 7 bpm compared with 55 ± 9 bpm (t = 5.01, p < 0.001).

The effect size for HRR1 was very large (Cohen’s d = 2.3). This means that the average trained participant recovered faster than almost every untrained participant: the two distributions overlapped only slightly. The trained group’s one-minute recovery was about 50 % greater than the untrained group’s.

5.4 Recovery relative to the rise in heart rate

Because the two groups started recovery from different heart rates, it is useful to express recovery as a proportion of the rise caused by exercise. In the trained group, heart rate rose by about 87 bpm above resting (from 59 to 146 bpm) and had recovered by 54 bpm, or about 62 % of this rise, after one minute. In the untrained group, heart rate rose by about 84 bpm (from 77 to 161 bpm) and had recovered by 36 bpm, or about 43 %, after one minute. Even when the different starting points are taken into account, the trained group recovered a much larger proportion of the rise in the first minute.

5.5 Individual variation

The group means hide considerable variation between individuals. In the trained group, HRR1 ranged from 43 to 66 bpm; in the untrained group, from 19 to 51 bpm. Two untrained participants recovered by 45 and 51 bpm, which is within the range of the trained group, while one trained participant recovered by only 43 bpm. These overlaps show that heart-rate recovery alone could not always classify an individual correctly as trained or untrained, even though the difference between the group means is large. The variation within each group may reflect differences in the type of training (endurance sports such as athletics and swimming may produce greater cardiovascular adaptations than football), as well as differences in body size, sex, sleep and genetics. With only ten participants in each group, it was not possible to test these factors, but they help to explain why Daanen et al. (2012) recommend using heart-rate recovery mainly to track changes in the same person.

6. Discussion

6.1 Explaining the faster recovery

The faster recovery of trained participants, especially in the first minute, is consistent with the physiology described in section 2. The steep fall in the first minute is caused mainly by the return of vagal activity, and training increases vagal tone, so trained participants would be expected to show a stronger and faster vagal reactivation. Daanen et al. (2012) describe heart-rate recovery in the first minute as reflecting mainly parasympathetic reactivation, and the large difference in HRR1 found here therefore suggests a real difference in autonomic control between the groups.

The larger stroke volume of trained participants is also likely to contribute. Because they can maintain cardiac output with fewer beats, their heart rate does not need to stay as high after exercise to supply the oxygen needed to repay the oxygen deficit and remove metabolites. The slower phase of recovery, after the first minute, depends more on the removal of adrenaline and metabolites; trained participants, who probably produced less lactate during the test because it was relatively easier for them, would also be expected to recover faster in this phase (Kenney, Wilmore and Costill, 2015).

6.2 Is the difference caused by training?

The data show a large and statistically significant difference between the groups, but this does not necessarily mean that training alone is responsible. Two kinds of alternative explanation must be considered.

The first concerns the design of the test. The step test was the same absolute workload for everyone, but for untrained participants it was relatively harder. Their heart rate at the end of exercise was 15 bpm higher, and several said the test felt hard. Daanen et al. (2012) note that heart-rate recovery is slower after more intense exercise. Part of the slower recovery of untrained participants might therefore be caused by the higher relative intensity of the exercise for them, rather than by a slower recovery mechanism. The analysis in section 5.4 partly addresses this, because the trained group still recovered a larger proportion of their rise in heart rate, but it does not remove the effect completely. Matching the relative intensity of the exercise, for example by asking each participant to exercise until their heart rate reached the same percentage of their predicted maximum, would give a fairer comparison.

The second concerns differences between the participants. The groups were not matched for sex, body mass, sleep or diet, and the study was not randomised: participants chose their level of activity themselves. People who choose to train regularly may differ from those who do not in other ways that affect heart rate, including genetic factors. Training status was also defined by self-report, which may not reflect actual fitness accurately. A stronger design would measure the same participants before and after a training programme, which would remove differences between individuals and show whether training actually causes faster recovery.

Considering both points, the results show that trained 16–18-year-olds recover substantially faster after this test, and the size of the difference and its concentration in the first minute strongly suggest that improved vagal reactivation due to training is an important part of the explanation. However, the design cannot fully separate this from the effects of relative exercise intensity and other differences between the groups.

6.3 Heart-rate recovery and health

Heart-rate recovery is also of interest beyond sport. In adults, a slow recovery of heart rate after exercise is associated with poorer cardiovascular health, and it is sometimes measured as part of clinical exercise testing. The participants in this study were healthy young people, and none of the untrained participants showed a recovery that would be a cause for concern; their slower recovery simply reflects a lower level of fitness. Nevertheless, the results illustrate the general principle that regular exercise improves the control of the heart by the autonomic nervous system, and that this improvement can be measured with simple equipment. This gives a positive message for health education: an improvement in heart-rate recovery is one of the measurable benefits that a student who starts training regularly might expect to see within a few weeks.

6.4 Practical implications

For coaches and physical education teachers, the results suggest that a simple step test with a heart-rate monitor can distinguish clearly between trained and untrained students: the difference in one-minute recovery was large and consistent. However, as Daanen et al. (2012) recommend, heart-rate recovery is likely to be most useful for tracking changes in the same person over time, under carefully standardised conditions, rather than for comparing individuals. A student who wants to monitor the effect of training could repeat the same test every few weeks, at the same time of day and under the same conditions, and look for an increase in HRR1.

7. Evaluation

7.1 Strengths

The protocol was carefully standardised: step height, stepping rate and duration were fixed with a metronome, recovery was measured in the same seated position, and testing took place at the same time of day with restrictions on caffeine, food and exercise. Chest-strap monitors gave precise readings and avoided the errors of counting the pulse by hand while heart rate was changing quickly. The pilot with the simulator helped to choose the recording times. The ethical procedures, including the PAR-Q, informed consent from participants and parents, the right to withdraw and anonymity, were appropriate for research with young people. The analysis checked the assumptions of the statistical tests, used a non-parametric test as a check, and reported an effect size as well as p-values.

7.2 Weaknesses and improvements

The most important weakness was that the step test was the same absolute workload for everyone, so it was relatively harder for untrained participants. This may have exaggerated the difference in recovery. A test matched to relative intensity, or measuring recovery after several different intensities, would show how much of the difference is due to training itself.

The second weakness was the cross-sectional design, comparing two different groups of people who were not randomly assigned and not matched for sex, body mass and other factors. A longitudinal study, measuring the same students before and after six to eight weeks of training, would provide much stronger evidence that training causes faster recovery.

Third, the sample was small, with ten participants in each group, from a single school. Although the difference was large enough to be clearly significant, a larger and more varied sample would show whether the results apply to other groups of young people, and would allow comparisons between males and females or between different sports.

Fourth, training status was defined by self-reported training frequency, which does not measure fitness directly and may be inaccurate. Measuring an indicator of aerobic fitness, such as performance in a multistage fitness test or an estimate of maximal oxygen uptake, would allow recovery to be compared with fitness as a continuous variable, which would be more informative than two groups.

Finally, the data in this exemplar are illustrative. In a real essay the results, and therefore the strength of the conclusions, would depend on the measurements actually made, which would probably show more variation than the data presented here.

7.3 Overall reliability of the conclusions

The conclusion that trained participants recovered faster in this test is strongly supported: the difference was large, statistically significant with two different tests, consistent across both one-minute and two-minute recovery, and very large in terms of effect size. The conclusion that training causes the difference is less certain, because of the matched-workload problem and the non-random groups. The physiological explanation, faster vagal reactivation, is consistent with the timing of the difference and with published research, but it was not measured directly. These different levels of certainty should guide how the results are used: as good evidence that trained students recover faster, and as reasonable but not conclusive evidence that training is the reason.

7.4 Unresolved questions

How quickly does heart-rate recovery improve when an untrained student starts training? Is the difference between trained and untrained students the same after exercise of equal relative intensity? And does heart-rate recovery change with hydration, sleep or heat, which could make a single measurement misleading?

8. Conclusion

Training status had a large effect on heart-rate recovery after a 2-minute step test in 16–18-year-olds. Trained participants finished the test with a lower heart rate (146 compared with 161 bpm), their heart rate fell by 54 bpm in the first minute compared with 36 bpm in untrained participants, and they returned to resting values within five minutes, while untrained participants did not. The differences were statistically significant and the effect size was very large (d = 2.3). Even when expressed as a proportion of the rise in heart rate, trained participants recovered much faster (about 62 % compared with 43 % in the first minute).

These results are consistent with the effects of training on vagal tone and stroke volume, and the concentration of the difference in the first minute points particularly to faster parasympathetic reactivation. Training status therefore affects heart-rate recovery to a large extent. However, because the same absolute workload was relatively harder for untrained participants, and because the groups differed in ways other than training, part of the difference may be due to these factors rather than to training itself. A longitudinal study with exercise matched to each person’s fitness would show more clearly how much of the faster recovery is caused by training.
