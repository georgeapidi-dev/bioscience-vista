1. Introduction

Vinegar is sold in supermarkets in Kenya in strengths from about 4 % to more than 10 % ethanoic acid, yet a strong vinegar does not taste or behave twice as acidic as a weak one. The reason is that ethanoic acid is a weak acid: only a small fraction of its molecules release hydrogen ions in water, so the hydrogen ion concentration, and therefore the pH, changes much less than the concentration of the acid itself.

Chemistry textbooks predict the pH of a weak acid with a simple approximation, pH = ½(pKa − log₁₀ c), where c is the concentration of the acid. This equation is used in almost every IB Chemistry course, but it rests on an assumption: that so little of the acid dissociates that the concentration of undissociated acid is effectively equal to c. I wanted to test this assumption experimentally, to find out how well the equation predicts real measurements and whether there is a concentration at which it begins to fail.

This essay investigates the research question:

How does the concentration of ethanoic acid (0.01–1.00 mol dm⁻³) affect its pH at 25 °C, and to what extent do measured values agree with the weak-acid approximation pH = ½(pKa − log₁₀ c)?

The first part of the question requires accurate pH measurements across a hundredfold range of concentration. The second part requires comparing the measurements with the approximation and with the exact solution of the equilibrium expression, and judging whether any differences are larger than the uncertainties of the experiment. The essay therefore combines a quantitative experiment with an evaluation of a theoretical model.

2. Background

2.1 Weak acids and the acid dissociation constant

An acid is a proton (H⁺) donor. A strong acid such as hydrochloric acid dissociates completely in water, so a 0.10 mol dm⁻³ solution contains 0.10 mol dm⁻³ of hydrogen ions and has a pH of 1.00. A weak acid dissociates only partially, and an equilibrium is set up:

CH₃COOH(aq) ⇌ CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H⁺(aq)

The position of this equilibrium is described by the acid dissociation constant, Ka = [H⁺][CH₃COO⁻] ÷ [CH₃COOH]. For ethanoic acid at 25 °C, Ka = 1.75 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³, so pKa = −log₁₀ Ka = 4.76 (Haynes, 2016). A small Ka means that the equilibrium lies far to the left: most of the acid remains as undissociated molecules.

2.2 Deriving the approximation

If a weak acid of concentration c dissociates to give x mol dm⁻³ of H⁺, it also gives x mol dm⁻³ of ethanoate ions, and c − x mol dm⁻³ of acid remains. Substituting into the expression for Ka gives:

Ka = x² ÷ (c − x)

This can be solved exactly as a quadratic equation. However, if x is very small compared with c, then c − x ≈ c, and the expression simplifies to Ka = x² ÷ c, so x = √(Ka c). Taking negative logarithms gives:

pH = ½(pKa − log₁₀ c)

This equation makes two testable predictions. First, a graph of pH against log₁₀ c should be a straight line with a gradient of exactly −0.500. Second, its intercept, at log₁₀ c = 0 (c = 1 mol dm⁻³), should equal ½ pKa, which is 2.38 for ethanoic acid (Atkins and de Paula, 2014).

2.3 When should the approximation fail?

The approximation depends on the fraction of acid that dissociates, called the degree of dissociation, α = x ÷ c. Using the approximation, α = √(Ka ÷ c), so α increases as the acid becomes more dilute. For a 1.00 mol dm⁻³ solution α is only about 0.4 %, but for a 0.01 mol dm⁻³ solution it is about 4 %. As α increases, the assumption that c − x ≈ c becomes less accurate. Because the approximation ignores the acid that has dissociated, it slightly overestimates the concentration of undissociated acid, and therefore overestimates [H⁺]. It should predict a pH that is slightly too low, and the error should grow as the solution becomes more dilute.

2.4 Other factors that affect pH measurements

Two further factors can make real measurements differ from theory. Firstly, the equilibrium expression strictly uses activities rather than concentrations. In solutions containing ions, attractions between the ions reduce their effective concentration, so the activity of H⁺ is slightly lower than its concentration. In dilute solutions of a weak acid there are very few ions, so this effect is small (Atkins and de Paula, 2014). Secondly, Ka depends on temperature, so measurements must be made at a controlled temperature. The pH meter itself also needs careful calibration, because a glass electrode responds to the activity of H⁺ and its response can drift.

2.5 How a pH meter measures pH

A pH meter measures the potential difference between a glass electrode, whose thin glass membrane is sensitive to hydrogen ions, and a reference electrode of constant potential. According to the Nernst equation, the potential of the glass electrode changes by about 59 mV for each change of one pH unit at 25 °C. The meter converts this potential into a pH reading, but it can only do so accurately after it has been calibrated with buffer solutions of known pH, which fix both the zero point and the slope of the response. Because the slope depends on temperature, the meter must also be used at the same temperature as the buffers, or corrected for temperature. These requirements explain several decisions in the methodology: two-point calibration at pH 4.00 and 7.00, which brackets the measured range, regular checks against a buffer, and a water bath at 25 °C (Atkins and de Paula, 2014).

3. Methodology

3.1 Choice of method

The pH of each solution was measured directly with a calibrated pH meter. An alternative would have been to determine the pH from a titration curve, at the half-equivalence point, but this would measure pKa rather than the pH of the acid at different concentrations. Direct measurement gives the quantity that the research question asks about, and a digital meter with a resolution of 0.01 gives the precision needed to detect small differences from the approximation.

3.2 Pilot with the titration simulator

Before preparing the solutions, I used the BioScience Vista titration simulator, which calculates the pH of ethanoic acid solutions exactly from the charge balance. Setting the concentration to values between 0.01 and 1.00 mol dm⁻³ and reading the initial pH before any base was added showed that the expected pH range was about 2.4 to 3.4, within the range of the meter’s calibration buffers. It also showed that the difference between the approximation and the exact value at 0.01 mol dm⁻³ would be only about 0.01, which told me that the meter would have to be carefully calibrated if any difference was to be detected.

3.3 Preparation of solutions

A stock solution of approximately 1 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid was standardised by titration against 0.100 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide using phenolphthalein, giving 1.00 mol dm⁻³. Solutions of 0.50, 0.25, 0.10, 0.05 and 0.01 mol dm⁻³ were then prepared by dilution with distilled water using 10.00, 25.00 and 50.00 cm³ volumetric pipettes and 100.00 cm³ volumetric flasks. The 0.01 mol dm⁻³ solution was made by diluting the 0.10 mol dm⁻³ solution tenfold. Each flask was inverted twenty times to mix.

3.4 Measurement

The pH meter was calibrated at the start of each session with buffer solutions of pH 4.00 and 7.00, and the calibration was checked with the pH 4.00 buffer after every six readings. All solutions were placed in a water bath at 25.0 ± 0.5 °C for ten minutes before measurement. For each solution, the electrode was rinsed with distilled water, rinsed with a small portion of the solution, and then placed in a fresh 50 cm³ sample. The reading was taken once it had remained stable for 30 seconds. Three separate samples of each solution were measured, working from the most dilute to the most concentrated to minimise carry-over.

3.5 Analysis plan

For each concentration, the mean and standard deviation of the three readings were calculated. The theoretical pH was calculated from the approximation and from the exact quadratic solution. pH was plotted against log₁₀ c, and the gradient and intercept of the best-fit line were found by linear regression, with their standard errors, so that they could be compared with the theoretical values of −0.500 and 2.38.

3.6 Controlled variables

Temperature was controlled at 25.0 ± 0.5 °C because Ka and the response of the electrode both depend on temperature. The same pH meter and electrode were used for all readings, and the electrode was always rinsed and left for the same time in each sample, so that any systematic error affected all readings equally. The same source of distilled water was used for all dilutions, and the solutions were measured within two hours of preparation to limit absorption of carbon dioxide from the air, which would form carbonic acid and lower the pH of the most dilute solutions.

3.7 Safety and environmental considerations

Ethanoic acid at 1 mol dm⁻³ is an irritant, so eye protection and gloves were worn and the stock solution was handled in a fume cupboard. Sodium hydroxide is corrosive. Waste solutions were combined, neutralised with sodium hydrogencarbonate and poured down the sink with plenty of water. Volumes were kept small to reduce waste.

5. Analysis

5.1 The effect of concentration on pH

As the concentration of ethanoic acid increased a hundredfold, from 0.01 to 1.00 mol dm⁻³, the pH fell from 3.39 to 2.38, a change of only about one pH unit. In terms of hydrogen ion concentration, this means that [H⁺] increased only about tenfold, from 4.1 × 10⁻⁴ to 4.2 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³. This is exactly the behaviour expected of a weak acid: because [H⁺] is proportional to the square root of c, a hundredfold increase in concentration gives only a tenfold increase in [H⁺].

5.2 Testing the predicted gradient and intercept

The plot of pH against log₁₀ c is a straight line (Figure 1). Linear regression gave a gradient of −0.505 ± 0.002 (standard error), with r² > 0.999, and an intercept of 2.378. The intercept agrees with the predicted value of ½ pKa = 2.38 to within the precision of the meter. The gradient is very close to the predicted −0.500, differing by 0.005, which is about 1 % of the theoretical value. This difference is about three times the standard error, so it may be real rather than due to random error, and its cause is considered in section 6.

5.3 Comparing individual measurements with the approximation

At every concentration the mean measured pH agreed with the approximation to within 0.01, the resolution of the pH meter (Table 2). The largest difference was at 0.01 mol dm⁻³, where the measured pH (3.39) was 0.01 higher than the approximation (3.380). This difference is in the direction predicted in section 2.3: the approximation slightly overestimates [H⁺] and so predicts a pH that is too low.

5.4 Comparing with the exact solution

The exact solution of the equilibrium expression, which does not assume that c − x ≈ c, gives a pH of 3.389 at 0.01 mol dm⁻³, almost exactly the measured value. At higher concentrations the exact and approximate values differ by less than 0.005. In other words, the measurements are slightly better described by the exact solution than by the approximation, but the difference between the two is so small within this range of concentrations that it is only just detectable with a meter of this resolution. The slightly steeper measured gradient (−0.505 rather than −0.500) is also consistent with this: because the approximation’s error is largest at low concentration, the measured line is pulled slightly upwards at the dilute end, making it marginally steeper.

5.5 Uncertainties

The uncertainties in concentration, propagated from the volumetric apparatus, were between 0.5 % and 1.1 % (Table 3). Because pH depends on ½ log₁₀ c, even a 1.1 % uncertainty in c changes the predicted pH by only about 0.002, which is much smaller than the resolution of the meter. The standard deviations of the three readings were between 0.006 and 0.015. The random uncertainty in the measurements is therefore larger than the uncertainty caused by the preparation of the solutions, and the limiting factor in this investigation is the precision of the pH meter.

5.6 The degree of dissociation

The degree of dissociation, calculated from the exact solution, rose from 0.42 % at 1.00 mol dm⁻³ to 4.08 % at 0.01 mol dm⁻³ (Table 2). This tenfold increase over a hundredfold dilution follows from α = √(Ka ÷ c): diluting the acid shifts the equilibrium to the right, because the product [H⁺][CH₃COO⁻] falls faster than [CH₃COOH] when all three are diluted, so more molecules dissociate to restore the value of Ka. This is an example of Le Chatelier’s principle. The measurements are consistent with this: if the degree of dissociation had stayed constant, the pH would have risen by two units over the range studied, instead of the one unit observed. The data therefore show directly that dilution increases the fraction of a weak acid that dissociates, even though it decreases the concentration of hydrogen ions.

6. Discussion

6.1 How well does the approximation work?

Within the range studied, the approximation pH = ½(pKa − log₁₀ c) works very well. It predicts the slope and intercept of the graph to within about 1 %, and each individual pH to within 0.01. The reason is that even at the lowest concentration studied, only about 4 % of the acid is dissociated, so treating the concentration of undissociated acid as equal to c introduces an error of only about 4 % in [HA], which becomes an error of about 2 % in [H⁺] and less than 0.01 in pH. This is comparable to the uncertainty of the measurements.

For practical purposes, such as calculating the pH of vinegar or of a solution used in a school laboratory, the approximation is therefore entirely adequate. A typical household vinegar, at about 0.8 mol dm⁻³, would be predicted to have a pH of 2.43, with an error from the approximation of less than 0.001.

6.2 Evidence that the approximation begins to fail

Although the approximation works well, the data also contain evidence of its limitations. The measured pH at 0.01 mol dm⁻³ is closer to the exact value (3.389) than to the approximation (3.380), and the measured gradient is slightly steeper than −0.500. Both are consistent with the approximation beginning to fail as the acid becomes more dilute. However, both effects are close to the precision of the measurements. A single additional reading could change the mean at 0.01 mol dm⁻³ by 0.01, so the evidence is suggestive rather than conclusive.

Theory predicts that the error would grow quickly at lower concentrations. Using the exact solution, the approximation would underestimate the pH by about 0.03 at 0.001 mol dm⁻³ and about 0.09 at 0.0001 mol dm⁻³, when about a third of the acid is dissociated. These errors would be easily measurable with this meter. Extending the investigation to lower concentrations is therefore the best way to show where the approximation fails, and it would turn the suggestive evidence in this essay into a clear test.

6.3 Other possible explanations for small differences

Three other factors could explain small differences between the measurements and theory, and they need to be considered before concluding that the approximation is responsible.

The first is the use of concentrations instead of activities. In more concentrated solutions, interactions between ions lower the activity of H⁺ below its concentration, which would make the measured pH slightly higher than predicted. However, a weak acid produces very few ions even at 1.00 mol dm⁻³ (an ionic strength of only about 0.004 mol dm⁻³), so this effect should be small, and the measured pH at 1.00 mol dm⁻³ (2.377) was actually very slightly lower than predicted.

The second is temperature. Ka changes slightly with temperature, so a difference of 0.5 °C between solutions could change the pH by a small amount. Because all solutions were kept in the same water bath, any such effect would be similar for all of them and would not change the gradient.

The third is calibration error. If the meter read consistently high or low, every value would be shifted by the same amount, changing the intercept but not the gradient. The intercept agreed closely with theory, which suggests that calibration was accurate, and checking the pH 4.00 buffer after every six readings showed a drift of no more than 0.01.

Considering all three, the small differences in the data are best explained by the approximation itself, as the size and direction of the difference at 0.01 mol dm⁻³ match the exact calculation.

6.4 Wider significance

The investigation illustrates a general principle in chemistry: approximations are tools whose validity depends on conditions. The weak-acid approximation is valid when α is small, which is when Ka ÷ c is small. A common rule of thumb is that the approximation is acceptable when less than 5 % of the acid dissociates, which for ethanoic acid corresponds to concentrations above about 0.007 mol dm⁻³. The results of this essay support this rule: at 0.01 mol dm⁻³, just inside the limit, the error was detectable but very small. Chemists who work with very dilute solutions, for example in environmental monitoring of acid rain, must therefore use the exact solution rather than the approximation.

6.5 Comparison with a strong acid

The behaviour of ethanoic acid contrasts sharply with that of a strong acid. For hydrochloric acid, which dissociates completely, pH = −log₁₀ c, so a graph of pH against log₁₀ c would have a gradient of −1.00, twice as steep as that found here. Across the same range of concentrations, the pH of hydrochloric acid would change by two units, from 2.00 to 0.00. The gradient of −0.505 found in this investigation is therefore direct experimental evidence that ethanoic acid is weak, and the agreement of the intercept with ½ pKa provides an independent check on the value of Ka from the data book. In this sense, the graph is a simple way to classify an unknown acid as weak or strong and to estimate its pKa from pH measurements alone: the intercept of 2.378 gives pKa = 4.76, the same as the data book value.

7. Evaluation

7.1 Strengths

The main strengths of the method were the standardisation of the stock solution, the use of volumetric glassware of high precision, careful calibration of the pH meter with regular checks, temperature control, and repeat measurements on fresh samples. Together these reduced both random and systematic error to the point where differences of 0.01 pH could be discussed. Comparing the data with both the approximation and the exact solution allowed a more meaningful evaluation than simply calculating percentage errors. The pilot with the simulator helped to predict the size of the effect being looked for and showed that very careful calibration would be needed.

7.2 Weaknesses and improvements

The most important limitation is the resolution of the pH meter (0.01), which is close to the size of the effects being studied in this concentration range. A meter with a resolution of 0.001, together with more readings at each concentration, would allow a more precise test.

The second limitation is the range of concentrations. Because the approximation only begins to fail significantly below 0.01 mol dm⁻³, the investigation could not show clearly where the failure occurs. Preparing solutions of 0.001 and 0.0001 mol dm⁻³ would provide a much stronger test, although these solutions are more sensitive to contamination by carbon dioxide from the air, which forms carbonic acid. Using freshly boiled and cooled distilled water and covering the samples would reduce this problem.

Third, only three readings were taken at each concentration. Five readings would give more reliable means and standard deviations, and would make the statistical comparison between the approximation and the exact solution more convincing.

Fourth, the value of pKa (4.76) was taken from a data book. Determining pKa experimentally from the half-equivalence point of a titration with the same equipment would make the comparison more self-consistent, as any systematic error in the meter would then affect both the measured pH and the prediction in the same way.

Finally, the data in this exemplar are illustrative. In a real essay, the analysis would depend on the student’s own measurements, which might show larger random errors and would need to be discussed accordingly.

7.3 Overall reliability of the conclusions

Taking the strengths and weaknesses together, the conclusions of this essay have different levels of certainty. The overall relationship between concentration and pH, and the agreement of the gradient and intercept with theory, are strongly supported, because they depend on six concentrations across a hundredfold range and are much larger than any of the identified uncertainties. The conclusion that the approximation is valid to within the precision of the meter between 0.01 and 1.00 mol dm⁻³ is also well supported. The suggestion that the approximation begins to fail at 0.01 mol dm⁻³ is the least certain part of the conclusion, because the effect is the same size as the resolution of the meter. Random error, rather than systematic error, sets the limit on what can be concluded, which is why more precise measurement and a wider range of concentrations are the most useful improvements.

7.4 Unresolved questions

How does the validity of the approximation compare for acids with different pKa values, such as methanoic acid (pKa 3.75), which dissociates more? And how much does the use of activities rather than concentrations matter in solutions to which a salt such as sodium chloride has been added?

8. Conclusion

The pH of ethanoic acid decreases as its concentration increases, but by much less than the concentration changes: a hundredfold increase in concentration lowered the pH by only one unit, because [H⁺] is proportional to the square root of concentration. A graph of pH against log₁₀ c was a straight line with a gradient of −0.505 ± 0.002 and an intercept of 2.38, in close agreement with the predictions of −0.500 and ½ pKa.

To a large extent, the weak-acid approximation pH = ½(pKa − log₁₀ c) agrees with the measured values: within this range of concentrations, every measured pH matched the approximation to within the resolution of the pH meter. There was some evidence of the approximation beginning to fail at 0.01 mol dm⁻³, where the measured value matched the exact solution better, and in the slightly steeper gradient, but these differences were close to the precision of the measurements. The approximation is therefore valid for practical purposes between 0.01 and 1.00 mol dm⁻³, and its limitations would only become clearly measurable at lower concentrations.
