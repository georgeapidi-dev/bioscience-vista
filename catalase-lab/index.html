Step 2 · Explore · 15 min

Task 1 – Temperature, pH and substrate concentration

C1.1.8 Effect of temperature

  • Below the optimum: as temperature rises, molecules gain kinetic energy → more frequent successful collisions between substrate and active site → more enzyme–substrate complexes → rate increases.
  • Optimum temperature: the temperature at which the rate is highest (≈37–40 °C for most human enzymes).
  • Above the optimum: vibrations break hydrogen and ionic bonds in the tertiary structure → active site changes shape → denaturation → rate falls steeply.
  • The curve is not symmetrical. At low temperatures the enzyme is inactive but not denatured.

Memory hookRise = collisions · Fall = denaturation

C1.1.8 Effect of pH

  • pH measures H⁺ concentration; each 1 unit change = 10× change in [H⁺].
  • Either side of the optimum, changes in [H⁺] alter the charges on R-groups in the active site → substrate binds less well → rate falls.
  • At extreme pH, ionic and hydrogen bonds in the tertiary structure break → denaturation.
  • Pepsin (stomach) ≈ pH 2; salivary amylase ≈ pH 7; trypsin (small intestine) ≈ pH 8.

Memory hookpH changes Charges → Changes binding

C1.1.8 Effect of substrate concentration

  • With a fixed amount of enzyme, more substrate → more frequent collisions with active sites → rate increases (steeply at first).
  • Eventually all active sites are occupied (saturated) → the curve plateaus; enzyme concentration is now the limiting factor.
  • NOS: these sketch graphs are models. You will test the temperature model with your own data in Step 3.

Memory hookMore substrate → more collisions … until every seat (active site) is taken.

Task 2 – Practical: effect of temperature on catalase

Test the model sketch graph with data. Catalase from potato breaks down hydrogen peroxide: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂. Run the simulator below, or carry out the real practical and type your results into the simulator's table, or press Use sample data.

Method (real practical and simulator follow the same steps)
  1. Put 5 cm³ of 3% hydrogen peroxide + 2 cm³ pH 7 buffer in a boiling tube in a water bath. Leave 5 min to reach temperature.
  2. Add five 1 cm³ potato cubes at the same temperature; seal with a bung and delivery tube into an upturned measuring cylinder of water.
  3. Record the volume of oxygen collected after 60 s.
  4. Repeat at 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 °C. Do three repeats at each temperature.
  5. Safety: wear eye protection; H₂O₂ is an irritant; take care with hot water.

Step 4 · Explain · 15 min

Task 3 – Calculating rates of reaction

C1.1.9 Measuring rates of enzyme-catalysed reactions

  • Rate = amount of product formed (or substrate used) ÷ time. Units, e.g. cm³ s⁻¹ or mg min⁻¹.
  • If you only measure the time to reach an end-point, rate = 1 ÷ time (units s⁻¹).
  • Catalase: measure the volume of oxygen (gas syringe or upturned measuring cylinder). Amylase: iodine test at intervals until blue-black no longer appears.
  • Initial rate: draw a tangent to the curve at t = 0 and find its gradient (Δy ÷ Δx). This is the fastest rate, before substrate is used up.
  • The curve flattens because the substrate is used up, not the enzyme.

Memory hookRate = change ÷ time · Initial rate = tangent at zero
