Addition polymers

Guiding question: How do thousands of small molecules join into one long chain?

A plastic bag is made of molecules thousands of atoms long, built from ethene. Under heat, pressure and a catalyst, the double bonds of ethene molecules open and link end to end, like paper clips joined into a long chain.

By the end of this lesson I will be able to

  • Describe addition polymerisation using ethene as an example.
  • Draw the repeat unit of a polymer from its monomer, and the monomer from a repeat unit.
  • Monomer: a small molecule with a C=C bond (e.g. ethene).
  • Polymer: a very long molecule made from many monomers.
  • In addition polymerisation, the C=C bonds open and monomers join. Only one product.
  • n CH₂=CH₂ → –(CH₂–CH₂)ₙ–
  • Take the monomer; change C=C to C–C; put bonds out to each side through brackets; add n.
  • Ethene → poly(ethene); propene → poly(propene); chloroethene → poly(chloroethene), PVC.
  • Working backwards: remove the brackets, make C–C into C=C.
The big ideaIn addition polymerisation, many alkene monomers join as their C=C bonds open, forming a long saturated chain with a repeating unit.

✏️ Worked example: Repeat unit of poly(chloroethene) from CH₂=CHCl
  1. Draw the two carbons; turn C=C into C–C.
  2. Keep the atoms on each carbon: H, H on one; H, Cl on the other.
  3. Add a bond out to each side through square brackets.
  4. Write n after the bracket: –[CH₂–CHCl]ₙ–.
